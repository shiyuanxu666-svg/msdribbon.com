Custom Ribbon Print Durability: Colorfastness, Rub-Fastness and Print Adhesion Testing for OEM Buyers

A printed ribbon that looks perfect on the approval sample can still fail in the market. The print rubs off onto a customer's sleeve, the ink cracks along a fold after three weeks in a gift box, the colour of the base fabric shifts under a retail window display, or a ribbon that was meant to be hand-washable bleeds into the product it was packaged with. None of these failures are visible at the sampling stage, which is precisely why they reach the customer rather than staying in the factory. For a brand owner or retail buyer commissioning custom ribbon, durability is not a finishing detail. It is a specification, and like any specification it has to be written down, agreed before production, and verified with a method that both sides understand. This guide covers the tests that actually predict how a printed ribbon will perform in the conditions it will be used in, how to choose the right method for a given end use, how to read a test report rather than relying on a pass or fail stamp, and how to write test requirements into a purchase order so they become enforceable rather than aspirational. It is written for procurement managers, brand owners and quality engineers who need to specify printed ribbon with confidence and defend the specification to a supplier.

The central idea is simple. There is no universal durability grade for ribbon. A ribbon used as a pull-tab on a gift box is exposed to very different mechanical and chemical stress from a ribbon used as a garment trim, a pet collar accent, or a ribbon on a bottle that will sit in a warm display window. A test that is entirely appropriate for one application can be meaningless for another, and the most common failure in ribbon quality control is not a bad test result but a test that was never relevant to the end use in the first place. So the process runs in a fixed order: establish the use conditions, map those conditions to the stresses the ribbon will experience, select the corresponding test methods, write the thresholds into the specification, and then verify the bulk production against those thresholds.

Why printed ribbon durability is a buyer-managed risk

Ribbon manufacturing has a structure that makes quality control the buyer's responsibility more often than most people expect. The base fabric and the printed surface are frequently produced in different places and then combined. Woven ribbons are finished and dyed, then wound, then printed or woven with a jacquard motif. Polypropylene and RPET ribbons may be printed after extrusion or after weaving. Edge finishing, using, tipping and folding all happen as separate operations, and each one introduces a new mechanical stress on the print. The upstream mills and dyeing houses are specialised operations with their own quality standards, and their output is the substrate on which your print sits. When something goes wrong, the failure is usually a system failure at an interface rather than a deliberate act by any single supplier.

This matters because the interfaces are where durability risk concentrates. A print can pass every adhesion test on the raw ribbon and still fail after the ribbon has been edge-treated, because the finishing process uses heat, tension and sometimes a hot blade. A dye can be perfectly colourfast and still bleed when the printed ribbon is exposed to a plasticiser in a specific packaging film. A ribbon can be lightfast in a laboratory xenon arc and still fade in a south-facing shop window with a different spectrum. The buyer is the only party who knows the full set of downstream conditions, because the buyer is the only party who knows how the finished product will be packaged, displayed, washed, shipped and handled.

The three failure families you are actually managing. Every ribbon durability complaint falls into one of three groups, and each has a different test discipline behind it. First, mechanical failure, where the print or the ribbon surface is abraded, scratched or cracked by friction, which includes rub-fastness, crocking and abrasion testing. Second, chemical and wet failure, where the print or the dye is attacked by water, detergent, perspiration, alcohol, oil or packaging plasticiser, which includes wet rubbing, wash fastness, sweat fastness and bleed testing. Third, light and environmental failure, where colour degrades under exposure to light, heat, humidity or ozone, which includes lightfastness and artificial perspiration testing. A programme that only tests one of these three will reliably surprise you in one of the other two.

It is also worth being clear about what durability testing can and cannot tell you. These tests are comparative and relative. A result is meaningful against a stated method, a stated instrument and a stated grading scale, and it is meaningful when compared against a defined reference. It is not an absolute guarantee of performance in a specific retail environment, because the lab cannot replicate every combination of light, heat, friction and time that a product encounters. What the tests give you is a defensible basis for comparison between suppliers, between constructions, and between an approval sample and the bulk delivery. That is genuinely valuable, and it is the basis on which you should make commercial decisions.

Step 1: Map the end use to the stresses the ribbon will meet

Before selecting a test method, write down what the ribbon will actually do. This sounds obvious, but the majority of underspecified ribbon programmes fail here because the specification is written as a material description rather than a use description. Material descriptions answer what the ribbon is made of. Use descriptions answer what will be done to it. The second question is what determines the required durability.

Consider four illustrative applications and what each one actually demands. A ribbon used to wrap a boxed gift that will be handled, carried and stored in ambient household conditions needs good rub-fastness and reasonable general colourfastness, and it needs print adhesion strong enough to survive the flexing involved in tying and untying a bow. A ribbon used as a garment trim on a linen shirt or a child's dress needs wash fastness at the temperature the garment will be laundered at, and it needs sweat fastness if it sits against skin. A ribbon used as a decorative element on a pet product needs wet rub-fastness and resistance to a mild detergent, because it will be washed regularly in a household sink. A ribbon applied to a fragrance bottle or a cosmetic package needs lightfastness and resistance to alcohol or solvent contact, since it may sit in a warm display in front of a shop window and be handled with alcohol-based product on the fingers.

Ask four specific questions for each application and write the answers into the specification. What mechanical contact will the printed surface see, and how much? What liquids will it encounter, and at what temperature and for how long? How much light exposure, of what kind, over what period? And what is the assembly method, because the moment of assembly is often the moment of maximum stress on a print.

One more variable deserves attention because it is frequently overlooked: the substrate underneath the print. A print on a smooth, densely woven satin behaves very differently from the same ink on a fuzzy, fibrous flannel, and the same ink on a coarse natural-fibre tape behaves differently again. The physical behaviour of the ribbon determines what is achievable. A factory producing a wide range of constructions will know which print systems work on which base, and a good supplier will tell you when a construction is a poor candidate for a high-durability print rather than accepting the order and delivering something that fails in the field. Asking that question early is one of the most valuable things a buyer can do, because it is much easier to change the ribbon construction than to change the packaging of a finished product that is already on shelf.

Step 2: The core test methods and what they actually measure

The international test methods for textiles are published as ISO standards, and the equivalents widely used in North America and Europe have very similar logic. The most important thing for a buyer is not to memorise standard numbers but to understand what each method simulates and what a result means. The standards are often referenced by number in purchase orders without anybody understanding the mechanism, and that produces two failures: requirements written for the wrong condition, and results compared against a threshold that was never properly defined.

Rub-fastness and crocking

Rub-fastness testing measures how much colour transfers from the ribbon surface to an adjacent white cloth when the two are rubbed together under specified conditions, either dry or wet. The principle is simple and the result is intuitive: a higher grade means less transfer. This is the test that predicts the everyday complaint that a printed ribbon marks the skin, discolours a light-coloured card, or leaves ink on the customer's hand.

The two variants matter and they are not interchangeable. Dry rub testing simulates contact with a dry adjacent surface and is the right test for ribbons in general gift packaging, gift sets and general decoration. Wet rub testing, usually performed with a dampened conditioning cloth, simulates contact with moisture and is the relevant test for anything that will be handled with damp hands, exposed to condensation, washed, or worn against skin. For ribbons that are hand-washed or used in apparel, wet rub is the more meaningful measurement, and it is frequently stricter. Some buyers specify both, which gives a fuller picture of the ribbon's behaviour across dry and damp conditions at a modest additional cost.

The conditions of the test should always be written into the requirement. The pressure of the rubbing contact, the number of strokes, the type and colour of the adjacent cloth and whether it is wetted all change the result. A requirement that says only "rub-fastness: grade 4" without specifying dry or wet is incomplete, and different laboratories will interpret it differently.

Wash fastness

Wash fastness testing assesses how well the print and the base colour survive a specified laundering cycle. The sample is washed under a defined condition, and the change in colour is graded against a reference scale. This test is essential for any ribbon that will be laundered, which covers apparel trims, gift sets intended to be washed, home textile accents and fabric-wrapped gift items. It is largely irrelevant to a ribbon that lives permanently in a dry gift box, which is why buyers sometimes over-specify here and pay for testing that adds no information.

What matters in a wash fastness specification is the condition, not just the grade. A wash performed at 30 degrees Celsius with mild detergent behaves very differently from one at 60 degrees with a strong detergent, and a ribbon required to survive the first may not survive the second. Specify the wash condition as part of the requirement, and specify whether the ribbon is washed alone or in company with other items, since the abrasion and soiling of a mixed load is a harsher and more realistic condition for a trim. If the ribbon will be ironed, that is a separate stress and a separate requirement, because heat from an iron is a common source of print failure on garment trims.

Lightfastness

Lightfastness testing measures the resistance of colour to exposure to light. In practice this is done by comparing the sample against a set of reference standards of known lightfastness under exposure in an instrument such as a xenon arc or a daylight simulator, then grading the exposure the sample survives. For ribbons this is most relevant for decorative and display applications, for ribbons used in home décor and textile accessories, and for anything that will sit in a lit retail environment or be shipped in a shop window.

Two practical points are worth making. First, lightfastness is generally a property of the dye in the base fabric as much as of the print ink, and a poorly chosen pigment in the base can limit the whole ribbon's performance. Second, a printed colour is judged against the colour that was approved, so the meaningful requirement is usually that the printed ribbon retains a specified relationship to the approved standard, not an abstract number. Where a product carries a brand colour that must remain recognisable, the sensible approach is to set a grading against the master standard and to check the printed ribbon rather than only the base.

Print adhesion and crocking of the print layer

Print adhesion is the bond between the ink film and the ribbon surface, and it is tested by a different family of methods than colour transfer. Adhesion is commonly graded on a scale where a high value means the print resists removal by a specified treatment, such as tape, cross-hatch cutting or a specified solvent wipe. Tape adhesion tests the resistance of the print to being lifted by an adhesive tape; cross-hatch testing assesses whether the print film separates from the substrate when the sample is cut in a grid pattern and the tape is pulled away; solvent testing checks the print's resistance to chemical attack.

Adhesion is the test that catches the most visible failure mode in retail. A print that lifts at a fold line, or a transfer print that peels at the edge of a bow, or an ink that softens and smears under handling, is an adhesion failure even if the colour itself is perfectly fast. It is worth noting that a print can pass adhesion testing on the flat ribbon and still fail after the ribbon has been folded and tied into a bow, because the fold creates a sharp curvature and the stress concentrates on the outer radius. This is why a factory that produces finished bows as well as flat ribbon will understand the difference, and why it is worth asking whether the print has been tested in the assembled form rather than only on the flat tape.

For woven motifs, where the design is produced by weaving rather than printed onto the surface, the durability question shifts to abrasion and snag resistance of the yarn itself rather than adhesion of an ink film. The same careful mapping applies, but the test to specify is different.

Bleed and staining in contact with other materials

Bleed testing checks whether the dye migrates from the ribbon to an adjacent wet material. It matters whenever a wet ribbon could touch something else, which includes a ribbon bundled with a garment in a wash, a ribbon packaged with a product that has a wet or solvent-based surface, and a ribbon used as a decorative wrap on a product that might be wiped with a damp cloth. It is also relevant where a ribbon is stored alongside absorbent material in a gift set, because a bleeding ribbon can mark the packaging and the product, which is a cosmetic failure that generates complaints even though the ribbon itself is technically compliant.

For sublimation and transfer-printed ribbons, a specific additional check is whether the print has been fully fixed. Under-dyed or under-fixed transfer prints show a characteristic behaviour where the colour lifts under heat and pressure, which can happen later in an assembly line or at a fulfilment centre, well away from the factory. A sensible requirement is a wet-rub or heat test on the bulk roll before it leaves the factory, precisely because the point of failure is often somewhere else entirely.

Artificial perspiration, oil and chemical contact

For ribbons in contact with skin, artificial perspiration testing is the relevant measure, using a synthetic sweat solution at a specified acidity applied for a specified duration. For ribbons used in hair accessories, where the product contacts the scalp and is exposed to styling products, oil and alcohol resistance matter. For ribbons used in food or cosmetic-adjacent packaging, the requirement becomes more specific still, and the buyer should be careful to distinguish between a requirement that the ribbon does not itself contact food and one where it does.

The principle to carry through all of these is that the testing solution should reflect the actual contact. A test using a solution that is more severe than reality costs money and may push a supplier toward an unnecessarily expensive print system, while a test that is milder than reality gives false comfort. Neither outcome serves the buyer well, so it is worth spending a little time getting the conditions right before the samples are cut.

Step 3: How to read a durability test report

Test reports are frequently read only for the word "PASS", which discards most of the information the document contains and makes it impossible to compare results over time or between suppliers. A report that is worth paying for states the following, and a buyer who has specified these points will get them without argument because they are standard practice for a competent laboratory.

The method used, with its full identifier and year of revision. Two versions of the same method can behave differently, and a comparison between a result from an older report and a newer one is not valid unless the method matches. The conditioning environment. Most textile conditioning requires a specified temperature and relative humidity before testing, usually around 20 degrees and 65 percent relative humidity, and specimens not properly conditioned give unreliable results. The test conditions in full. For a rub-fastness test this means the dry or wet condition, the number of strokes and the load. For a wash test it means the temperature, the number of cycles, the detergent and the drying method. The grading scale and its direction. Some scales run from 1 to 5 where 5 is best, others are written as 1 to 5 where 1 is best, and reading a result backwards is an easy and costly mistake. The numerical grade for each specimen. Individual specimen results alongside the mean. This is where a report becomes genuinely useful, because a mean can hide a weak specimen, and a ribbon with four good results and one poor result is a quality control signal about process consistency rather than about the material. Any deviation from the standard method. A modified test is not the same test, and it should be identified. The identity of the sample. Which colour, which lot, which production date, and whether it came from the approval sample or the bulk production.

There is one more thing a good report should tell you and that is usually missing: whether the tested material is representative. A test performed on the approval sample tells you about the approval sample. Only a test performed on bulk production, drawn from the finished rolls, tells you about what the customer is receiving. The correct practice is to draw specimens from the bulk production run, typically from the beginning, middle and end of the run, since a single specimen taken at one end of a long run proves very little. This is the point at which the process becomes a quality control system rather than a supplier's assurance, and it is where most of the value is.

Step 4: Writing durability requirements into the purchase order

The reason most durability problems become disputes is that the requirement was never written down in a form both parties agreed to. A purchase order that says "printed ribbon, red, 25mm" and a purchase order that says the same thing plus a set of test conditions with named methods and numeric thresholds are different instruments. The first is a description; the second is a specification that can be verified.

A well-formed durability clause names the test, the condition and the threshold, in that order. The wording matters because it determines whether a supplier can meet it and whether it can be checked. Where the buyer is uncertain of the appropriate condition, it is better to state the intended end use and require the supplier to propose the method, which puts the process knowledge where it belongs and still produces a written, agreed and testable requirement.

The same clause should establish several procedural points that are cheap to write and expensive to argue about later. It should state which stage the testing happens at, and whether the supplier is required to test bulk production before shipment or whether the buyer tests on receipt. It should state the sampling plan, so that the number of rolls and the number of specimens per roll are defined rather than negotiated after the container arrives. It should state the retest procedure, including what happens if the result fails, whether the supplier has the right to present further production for testing, and how a failed lot is dispositioned, since this is where a genuine dispute is most likely to occur. It should state which parties pay for testing when a result is disputed and the outcome determines a substantial volume of goods, because in an international trade this point is regularly left unstated. And it should state the effective date from which the requirement applies, so that a change in specification does not create a dispute about goods already in production.

It is worth noting that durability requirements should be set at the programme level rather than at the order level, and then referenced in individual purchase orders. A ribbon specification that lives in a shared document, versioned and referenced by number in each order, avoids the most common failure of custom ribbon programmes, which is that different buyers, different buyers' assistants and different planners each apply a slightly different understanding of the same programme. The reference should point to the test method, the condition and the threshold, so that there is no room for interpretation.

What a capable supplier looks like on durability

You can tell a great deal about a supplier's attitude towards print durability before you ever see a test report, simply by asking a few well-chosen questions. A supplier that treats durability seriously will ask what the end use is, which is the single clearest indicator that they understand the problem. They will tell you which print systems are appropriate for the construction and the use, and they will sometimes recommend a different construction, which can look like a sales tactic but is usually the opposite. They will tell you when a particular colourway or a particular ink cannot meet a demanding requirement, and they will have an established relationship with an accredited testing laboratory rather than improvising the test themselves.

FAQ — Custom ribbon print durability and colorfastness testing

What is the difference between rub-fastness and crocking when testing printed ribbon?

They are related but not identical, and the distinction matters when you write the requirement. Rub-fastness is the general term for testing how much colour transfers from the ribbon to an adjacent white cloth when the two surfaces are rubbed together under a defined load for a defined number of strokes, and it is commonly performed in both dry and wet variants. Crocking more specifically refers to the transfer of colour to an adjacent surface in ordinary handling, and the term is used loosely in commercial conversation to mean the same practical behaviour. The reason to distinguish them in a purchase order is that dry and wet rub are genuinely different tests and only one of them may be relevant to your use. A ribbon on a gift box needs dry rub. A ribbon worn against skin or hand-washed needs wet rub, which is the stricter test. If the requirement simply says rub-fastness with a grade and no dry or wet condition, a supplier and a laboratory may interpret it differently, and you lose the ability to compare results over time.

Which durability test does a custom ribbon actually need for a gift packaging application?

For a ribbon that wraps or decorates a boxed gift and is handled in dry household conditions, dry rub-fastness is the primary requirement, along with reasonable general colourfastness and adequate print adhesion so that the design survives being tied, untied and handled. Wash fastness is largely irrelevant for a ribbon that will never be laundered, and lightfastness only matters if the gift is displayed in a lit retail window for a sustained period. A useful additional check for packaged goods is a bleed or staining test against the packaging material, because a ribbon that transfers colour onto a white card or a tissue wrap generates complaints about the presentation even though the ribbon itself has passed every test that was specified. The common mistake is over-specifying, ordering a full panel of tests for an application that only experiences one or two stresses, and paying for assurance that tells you nothing.

How do I specify colourfastness requirements for a printed ribbon without over-complicating the purchase order?

Name the method, the condition and the threshold, in that order, and add the sampling plan. For example, wet rub-fastness to a named standard with a stated number of strokes, graded to a stated value on a stated scale, tested on specimens drawn from the bulk production run at a stated sampling rate, with a stated procedure for retesting a failed lot. If you are not certain of the correct condition, it is more effective to state the intended end use and require the supplier to propose the method, since the supplier holds the process knowledge about what is achievable on a given construction, and this still produces a written and testable requirement. The single most important thing is that the requirement be specific enough that two different laboratories would run the same test, because a threshold without a method cannot be defended or meaningfully compared.

Does testing the approval sample tell me anything about the bulk production?

It tells you about the approval sample, which is a useful but limited result. Approval samples are typically produced on a small piece of equipment or in a small lot, sometimes with a different print system or a slightly different finishing route than the bulk run, and they are frequently selected because they are good examples of the target. A strike-off that passes every test tells you that the colour and construction are right and that the process is capable, which is a genuine and important result, but it does not confirm that three thousand metres produced over three weeks on a different machine met the same standard. The reliable practice is to draw test specimens from the bulk production itself, taken from the beginning, the middle and the end of the run, and to test those. Where the programme justifies it, we recommend testing bulk production before shipment rather than on receipt, since the point of testing is to prevent a problem reaching the customer, not to document it afterwards.

Can a print that passes adhesion testing still fail after the ribbon is made into a bow?

Yes, and it is a common cause of field failures. Adhesion is tested on a flat strip of ribbon, but assembly concentrates stress. Folding the ribbon into a bow creates a sharp curvature and puts the print under tension on the outer radius, and the edge finishing operation that seals the raw edge of a woven ribbon uses heat and sometimes a hot blade, both of which can degrade an ink film that passed a flat-strip test. Tying and untying repeatedly puts a flexing cycle through the print at exactly the point where the ribbon turns. This is why it is worth asking a supplier who makes both flat ribbon and finished bows whether the print has been tested in the assembled form. It also means the assembly method and the materials used in it belong in the specification, because the print is only as durable as the weakest step in the process that assembles it.

What does wash fastness tell me about a ribbon used as a garment trim?

Wash fastness tells you how well the print and the base colour survive laundering, and for a garment trim that is one of the most important tests you can specify. The result is only meaningful when the wash condition is stated, because a wash at 30 degrees with a mild detergent and a wash at 60 degrees with a strong detergent are different tests with different outcomes, and the ribbon should be required to survive the condition that the finished garment will actually be laundered under. Whether the ribbon is washed alone or with other garments also changes the result, since the tumbling and soiling of a mixed load is a harsher and more realistic environment for a trim than an isolated specimen. If the garment will be ironed, specify that separately, because heat from an iron is a distinct stress on the print that a wash test does not capture, and it is a frequent failure point on woven trims and printed ribbons applied to apparel.

Is RPET recycled polyester ribbon as durable as virgin polyester ribbon for printed applications?

In general it is, and there is no need to assume a performance penalty. RPET ribbon is made from recovered and recycled polyester, and the fibre chemistry and the resulting filament properties are comparable to virgin polyester, so colourfastness and print adhesion performance are essentially equivalent when the same yarn, the same dye route and the same print system are used. The variables that actually determine durability are the construction, the dye quality, the print method and the substrate finish, and these are independent of whether the feedstock is recycled. What recycled material does change is the story behind the product, which matters for brands with a sustainability commitment and for market access requirements in some jurisdictions. At MSD Ribbon we produce RPET alongside conventional polyester, and we would note that the durability conversation for an RPET programme is the same as for a conventional one: the use conditions determine the specification, and the construction determines what is achievable.

How often should custom ribbon be durability tested during an ongoing production programme?

Test the bulk production of every lot, because this is the test that has commercial value, and it does not need to be an expensive process if the sampling plan is agreed in advance. For an established programme with a stable construction and a proven print method, testing the first roll of each production run plus additional specimens drawn at defined intervals through the run is normally sufficient, since the purpose is to detect a change in the process rather than to re-characterise a material whose characteristics are already known. A more demanding regime is appropriate when something has changed, whether that is the dye lot, the print system, the base fabric source, the finishing route or the physical construction, and in that case the first run after the change should be tested more comprehensively. The key discipline is that the testing plan is written into the purchase order, so the sampling rate, the methods and the thresholds are all agreed before the goods are made rather than negotiated when a result is in dispute.

Who should pay for durability testing when a bulk order fails the agreed standard?

The default position should be that the supplier bears the cost of retesting, because the requirement was agreed in writing before production and the goods did not meet it. That is why the retest procedure needs to be set out in the purchase order rather than left to be worked out afterwards. The purchase order should specify the procedure to be followed when a result fails, which includes a retest on new specimens drawn from the same lot using the same methods, the point at which the result is deemed final, and the disposition of the lot, which is normally replacement or credit rather than acceptance. Where the failure turns out to be attributable to something outside the supplier's control, such as an error in the specification that was corrected mid-production, the position can reasonably be different, and the purchase order is the place to set the mechanism for handling that situation. What should never happen is a dispute about who pays for the test, because that argument stops the two parties from dealing with the issue that actually matters, which is why the supply of ribbons by a compliant factory through a properly written specification is the cheapest form of insurance available to a buyer in this category.

What should a buyer do when a durability failure is discovered after the ribbon has already been assembled into finished bows?

Separate the question of what to do about the current delivery from the question of what to change for the next one, because mixing them leads to a poor outcome for both parties. For the current delivery, establish whether the failure is a materials defect, a process defect or a specification gap, and this is done by testing the remaining bulk ribbon against the same agreed methods rather than by examining the finished bows alone, since a failure that appears on the finished product but not on the flat ribbon points to assembly as the cause. Any credit, replacement or rework should then follow from that finding. For the next delivery, the corrective action depends entirely on the cause. A materials defect calls for a change of dye lot or base fabric, a process defect calls for tightening control at the printing or finishing step, and a specification gap means the requirement was never written down and needs to be formalised. Recording which of the three applies is what stops the same failure recurring on the following order.
