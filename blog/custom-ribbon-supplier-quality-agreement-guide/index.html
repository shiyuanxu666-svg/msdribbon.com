Custom Ribbon Supplier Quality Agreement (SQA) Guide for OEM Buyers

Most buyers of custom ribbon believe the quality conversation with a supplier happens in the sample approval stage, and in a sense it does. It is there that the shade is agreed, the width is signed off, the print is approved and the bow tension is settled. The problem is that a verbal agreement made over a physical sample leaves no record, and six months later, on the third reorder, the supplier has delivered a ribbon that is slightly the wrong shade, the print registration is a half-millimetre off, and the spools are packed at the wrong metre length. At that point nobody can establish what was actually agreed, because the agreement was made in a meeting room and never written down. This is not a supplier behaving badly. It is the predictable consequence of running a custom programme without a quality agreement, and it is the single most common reason that good supplier relationships in the ribbon category deteriorate over time. A supplier quality agreement, usually shortened to SQA, is the document that fixes that. It sits alongside the purchase order rather than replacing it, and it does one specific job: it converts the mutual understanding you reached during sampling into a controlled, versioned, auditable set of obligations on both sides. It is the difference between a programme that works because everyone is being reasonable, and one that works because everyone is working to the same written standard.

It is worth being precise about what an SQA is and is not, because the term is used loosely and buyers sometimes expect something they do not want. It is not a second purchase order, it is not a substitute for a technical specification, and it is not a quality certificate. The specification describes the product. The purchase order describes the commercial transaction. The SQA describes the relationship: how quality will be defined, how it will be evidenced, who is responsible for what, what happens when something goes wrong, and how the relationship is governed over time. That framing matters because a good SQA is actually a shared document. A buyer who drafts it as a list of unilateral demands will get it signed and then ignored, which is worse than having no agreement at all, because the existence of the document creates a false sense of control. The version that works is the one that both sides recognise as a fair statement of how quality will actually be handled on the factory floor, including the difficult parts, particularly the parts where the buyer's own specification was incomplete.

There is also a commercial reason to want one, and it is not a soft one. Custom ribbon programmes involve a significant amount of tooling and knowledge transfer: the artwork, the print programme, the colour formula, the edge treatment, the bow tension, the packing format. Each of those is a piece of information that lives with one party. When the relationship is healthy, that information flows and the reorder costs less and the quality is more consistent. When the relationship is not documented, every reorder is a fresh negotiation, and the supplier is incentivised to quote it as a fresh job, which means a fresh price and a fresh set of assumptions. A well-drafted SQA with a clear change-control clause and a review cadence preserves that knowledge inside the relationship, which is where the value of a long-term programme actually lives.

When an SQA is worth the effort, and when it is not

An SQA pays for itself quickly on any programme with repeat orders, more than one construction, or a compliance requirement, because each of those introduces a way for the written specification and the delivered goods to diverge. It is worth the effort when you are placing orders for more than one market or more than one buyer specification, since that is where the temptation to substitute a standard construction for a bespoke one always appears. It is worth the effort when you have been caught by a supplier change you were not told about, and it is worth the effort when the goods go into an assembly operation where a defect is not visible until the finished product is built.

It is less obviously worth the effort in a genuinely one-off order where the value at risk is small relative to the effort of drafting and negotiating. A single order for a hundred metres of ribbon for a trade show does not need a sixteen-clause agreement. The honest way to think about the threshold is to compare the cost of drafting the agreement against the cost of one argument about what was agreed. In a made-to-order programme where the arguments involve colour tolerances, print registration, packing format and inspection levels, one unresolved argument usually costs more than the drafting. The practical middle path is to draft a short version for small or trial orders, with the specification attached and a reference to a master agreement where one exists, and to expand it as the programme grows.

It is also important to understand the relationship between the SQA and the other documents a ribbon programme runs on, because buyers frequently treat these as overlapping and end up with contradictions. The technical specification tells the supplier how to build the ribbon, with numeric tolerances for width, colour, construction and print. The artwork or print file controls the decoration, and needs its own version control. The inspection instruction tells the third-party agency what to check and against which reference, and it is derived from the specification rather than written independently. The AQL and sampling plan sit inside the inspection instruction. The purchase order carries the commercial terms. The SQA is the framework that says which document governs which decision, who approves changes to which document, and what the consequences are when one of them is breached. When a buyer has two documents saying different things about width tolerance and neither is subordinate to the other, the disagreement is procedural before it is technical, and the SQA is what prevents it.

The eight sections a working SQA contains

There is no single mandated structure, but a ribbon SQA that works reliably contains the following eight areas. Each is discussed below, and the order reflects how much time each one usually saves.

1. Scope, applicability and effective date

State which products, which constructions and which order types the agreement covers, which purchase orders it applies to, whether it applies to all your suppliers or one, and the date from which it takes effect. For a multi-supplier buyer, say explicitly whether the same document is used for every supplier and how departures are recorded. This section is short and it is routinely omitted, and the omission creates a recurring argument: the supplier will say the agreement applies to a specific product range, you will say it applies to everything they make for you, and neither of you can point to a line that settles it.

2. Controlled document references

This is the section that does the most work and it is the one that must be built with real care. The purpose is to make it unambiguous, at any future moment, which exact document set the goods were supposed to be made against. That requires three things per referenced document: a unique identifier, a version or revision number, and a controlled location where the current version lives and where superseded versions are retained. For a ribbon programme that list typically includes the product specification sheet, the artwork or print file, the colour reference, the physical standard sample with its seal number, the packing format sheet, and the inspection instruction. The colour reference deserves special attention because it is usually the least formalised of these. A Pantone reference alone is not a colour specification for a dyed ribbon, because dye lots vary and Pantone references relate to a specific substrate and print application. What works is a sealed physical sample, photographed under a stated light source, with a stated delta E tolerance, kept by both parties, and used as the reference for every subsequent lot. When you can point at a sealed sample with a number on it, a colour argument becomes a comparison rather than a debate.

3. Material and construction requirements

This section states what the ribbon is made of and how it is built: fibre content with the required declaration wording, construction type, nominal width with tolerance, weave or finish, edge treatment, wire gauge and wire count and position for wired ribbon, colourfastness requirements against a stated method, shrinkage allowance and the conditions under which it is measured, and any substrate restrictions such as a specified recycled content percentage or a prohibition on a particular chemistry. Where the goods will touch skin, food or an infant product, the material section is where those requirements belong, and the SQA should name the test method and the test standard rather than the property, because "meets REACH requirements" is not a specification and "passes EN 71-3 migration testing on the finished article, report issued by an accredited laboratory" is.

4. Decoration and finishing requirements

This covers everything applied to the base material: the print method, the number of colours, the minimum coverage or ink-to-substrate ratio, the registration tolerance expressed in millimetres, the minimum gauge of the base fabric to allow the print method to hold, bleed-through and crocking limits, foil weight and adhesion where hot foil or metallic transfer is used, and the edge treatment in detail, which for ribbon means selvedge, hemmed, wired, tipped or cut. For bows and pre-tied assemblies it covers the component list, the wire and cord specification, the pull or tensile requirement in newtons or kilograms, the finished length and width tolerances, and the number of components per unit. The tension requirement is worth specifying as a number with a test method because it is the single most common source of complaint in the bow category, and a bow that pulls apart in the hands of a retail customer is a returns problem rather than a manufacturing problem.

5. Packaging, labelling and shipment identification

The packing format is part of the product, and it is the section most often missing from the specification and then most often disputed. It should state the reel or spool type, the metres per reel, the number of colours per carton, the reels per carton, the carton dimensions and gross weight tolerance, the carton material and print, the label content and its placement, the barcode format and where it is applied, the pallet specification and how many cartons per pallet, and the shipment marking. For a buyer who assembles at volume, the reel count and the metres per reel drive labour planning directly, so a change of reel format between shipments creates a real cost even though the ribbon is identical. Making it a controlled item in the agreement removes the argument entirely.

6. Quality control, acceptance and inspection

This section references the inspection instruction rather than repeating it, and states that the inspection instruction, together with the defect classification and the AQL and sampling plan it contains, forms part of the agreement. It states the inspection level and the point in production at which the inspection occurs, the requirement that the goods will not be shipped before the result is issued and before any failed lot has been dispositioned, and the requirement that the report will contain measurements rather than only a verdict. It should also name who pays for the inspection, which for a new supplier is usually the buyer, and should state that the cost moves to the supplier where the inspection fails, which is the clause that makes the inspection programme economically self-funding over time. Where your own incoming inspection at your warehouse duplicates part of this, say so, and say that a lot which passes the factory inspection and fails your incoming inspection is still a non-conformance.

7. Non-conforming material, CAPA and remedies

This is the section that gets exercised, and it is the one that should be the most detailed and the most neutral. It should cover how a non-conformance is reported and to whom, the containment requirement, the CAPA procedure and its timeline, the permitted dispositions of rework, replacement, credit or acceptance as a concession, the cost allocation for each, the requirement for re-inspection of reworked goods, and the escalation ladder described in more detail below. What matters most in practice is that this section describes a process rather than a punishment, because a supplier who reads it as a threat will manage the paperwork and not the goods, and a supplier who reads it as a shared process will actually fix the process.

8. Governance: audits, changes, records and escalation

The final section makes the agreement durable. It covers audit rights and notice periods, the notification obligation and notice period for material changes, the retention period for production and QC records and the requirement that they be available for a defined period after the last shipment, the escalation path and named contacts at both parties, the circumstances in which the agreement is reviewed, and the term and termination provisions including what happens to goods already in production when the relationship ends.

Defect tolerance: how to write numbers that survive contact

The difference between a quality agreement that works and one that does not is almost always the specificity of the tolerances. An SQA full of language such as "good quality", "consistent appearance" or "to be agreed" will be read exactly as written, which is to say not at all, and every dispute will default to whoever remembers the sample approval meeting best. The discipline is to replace every adjective with a measurement or an explicit reference.

For width, the pattern is a nominal value with a plus or minus tolerance, measured at a stated number of positions across the reel, and it should state whether the measurement applies before or after edge finishing, since the two differ on some constructions. For colour, the pattern is a delta E range measured against the sealed standard sample under a stated light source, with an explicit statement that normal dye-lot variation within the limit is accepted, so that legitimate variation is not later treated as a defect. For print, the pattern is a registration tolerance in millimetres, a minimum coverage or ink ratio where rubbing resistance matters, a bleed-through limit for narrow and light constructions, and a colour count. For shrinkage, a percentage with the conditions stated, which for a ribbon that will be laundered or heat-pressed is a genuinely load-bearing number rather than a formality. For bow assemblies, a pull strength in newtons with a defined test method and the number of pulls required to pass.

Then the classification. The agreement should define critical, major and minor defects in the terms described in the dedicated inspection guidance, because the classification is what the AQL is applied to, and the AQL is meaningless without it. Linking the three documents explicitly, specification gives the tolerances, classification gives the severity, AQL gives the decision rule, is the architecture that makes an inspection result enforceable rather than advisory.

CAPA: the clause that separates an agreement from a wish list

Corrective and preventive action is where a quality agreement earns its keep, and it is where most agreements are thin. The structure that works has five stages, each with a timing requirement attached.

Containment. The non-conforming quantity is identified, quarantined and prevented from shipping, and the buyer is notified in writing within a defined period, typically within twenty-four hours. The key word is prevented: containment that occurs after the goods have been loaded does not contain anything.

Root cause analysis. The supplier investigates and reports in writing, using a recognised method rather than a guess. Five whys, a process or cause-and-effect diagram, or a failure mode and effects analysis on the affected process are all acceptable. The standard to apply when judging the report is whether it identifies a cause that is actually capable of producing the observed defect, because a supplier who reports that the operator was careless has not finished the analysis.

Corrective action. The specific change to the process, machine, method, material, or training that will prevent recurrence. This is where the report either becomes useful or becomes theatre, and the test is whether it is a change to a system or a change to a person. Rewriting the work instruction for the print line, adding a second operator check at the winding station, adding a colour-difference check at the folding step and re-calibrating the print registration sensor are all system changes. Training the operator more carefully is usually a person change, and it will work for a while.

Verification of effectiveness. Evidence that the corrective action worked. In practice this means a defined number of consecutive lots that pass inspection, or a re-inspection of the affected order, or both, and it should be stated as a number so it cannot be satisfied by a single good run.

Preventive action. Whether the same cause could affect other products, constructions, orders or customers, and if so what is being changed to prevent that. This is the stage that is almost always omitted, and it is the stage that converts a one-order fix into a supplier improvement. A shade that drifted because of a dye-lot issue on one colour will drift on another colour next season unless something changes in the dye-lot control process, and the preventive action step is where that gets caught.

Attach a closure deadline, specify the evidence required to close the action, and state that closure is a condition of continued business rather than an optional courtesy. Buyers often want to specify a fixed number of days for every CAPA, and this is worth resisting, because a seven-day deadline on a genuine root cause investigation reliably produces a rushed and wrong answer, while a well-diagnosed fix that takes three weeks is worth far more than a fast wrong one.

Traceability and change control: the clauses that pay off later

These two are the most commonly omitted and among the most valuable, because they are the protection you have when a problem surfaces months after the goods have been consumed and you can no longer send someone to the factory.

Traceability means that any finished carton can be tied back to the fibre source, the dye lot, the print plate or screen, the production shift, the finishing line and the QC records for that lot. The supplier must be able to demonstrate this in a format you can actually read, which usually means a carton label carrying the lot identifiers and a production record that maps those identifiers to the QC results. This is not theoretical for ribbon. A shade that arrives slightly off in one shipment and correct in the next usually comes from a different dye lot, and without dye-lot traceability you cannot prove it, which means you cannot get a remedy, which means you absorb the cost. It also matters for recall: if a compliance issue emerges in a resin or a chemical, the question that determines your exposure is how quickly you can identify which shipments are affected, and traceability is the only thing that makes that answer available.

Change control means the supplier will not change anything material without written notice and your written approval. The critical parameter is the notice period, and it needs to be long enough to be useful. Sixty to ninety days is defensible for a change to fibre source, dye house, print line, finishing route, machinery or sub-supplier, because those changes need to be assessed against your own specification and, in the compliance case, re-tested. A one-week notice is useless, since by then the goods are already made. And the agreement needs to define what counts as a material change, because without a definition the clause is argued. A change of thread supplier, a change of print cylinder vendor, a change of tipping adhesive, a change of winding machine and a change of carton supplier all sound minor and none of them are. Put the list in the agreement, so the question never gets asked in the wrong month.

Audit rights versus factory visits

These are different instruments and the agreement should provide for both, because they serve different purposes and have different costs. A factory visit is informal and relational. You see the operation, you talk to the people who run the line, you look at how work is actually organised, and you come away with an understanding that no document will give you. The main risk to the buyer is that a visit that is only ever a social occasion tells you nothing. A visit should have a short written agenda agreed in advance, covering the specific lines and processes that touch your goods.

An audit is formal and evidentiary. It is an assessment against a written standard, producing a report with findings, a classification of major and minor findings, required corrective actions and a closure date. The clause should specify notice periods, frequency, scope, who may attend and whether you may bring your own staff or a third-party agency, and what must be made available during the audit, including process and in-process inspection records, calibration and maintenance records, raw material certificates, test reports, and non-conforming material records with their disposition. Two clauses carry disproportionate weight. The first is the right to audit a sub-supplier where a significant part of the product is bought in, because in a sourced construction the sub-supplier's process is your process. The second is a prohibition on refusing an audit on confidentiality grounds in a way that prevents inspection of the areas relevant to your goods, since without that, a buyer with a large customer sometimes cannot see anything. The main deterrent value of an audit right is that a supplier who knows it can be scheduled and used to disqualify a sub-supplier behaves differently in the ordinary course of business, long before anyone schedules anything.

How the SQA interacts with your other ribbon documents

A sensible SQA is short on duplication and long on precedence, which is not the same as being short. The specification holds the technical numbers. The artwork file holds the decoration. The inspection instruction holds the defect classes, the AQL and the sampling plan. The SQA holds the rules of the relationship, and it states clearly which document wins when they disagree. That precedence clause is worth more than it appears, because in practice the documents will drift apart over time, particularly the specification, which gets updated whenever a new colour or a new construction comes along, while the artwork file is versioned by whoever made it last and the inspection instruction is updated by the agency.

Make the update path explicit. When the specification changes, who tells the factory, who confirms the reference samples are still current, does the inspection instruction need to change with it, and does the AQL stay the same? The single most common finding in a well-run ribbon programme is that the physical standard sample no longer matches the current specification, because the specification was revised twice and the sample was never replaced. Put the review trigger in the agreement and the problem disappears.

Term, review and the escalation ladder

Treat the SQA as a living document with a formal annual review. An initial term of one to three years is normal, with revisions handled through a change log rather than a rewrite, so the history stays intact and you can always establish which version applied to which order. Beyond the annual review, review immediately after any significant CAPA, after any change in construction, print method or market, after any change in your own product specification, and after any change in the sub-supplier base. The review should be a scheduled conversation rather than a document refresh, because the value of the review is in discussing what failed and what to change, not in re-reading clauses that have not moved. Useful agenda items are the drift in defect rates by class, the number and age of open CAPAs, the audit findings from the last cycle, any change in sub-suppliers, the current dye-lot range for your standard colours, whether the AQL and inspection level still match the commercial value of the goods, and whether the standard sample is still the current one.

The escalation ladder should be defined in advance, and it should be proportionate. An isolated incident triggers containment and CAPA. A failure rate above an agreed threshold over a rolling period triggers enhanced monitoring and additional inspection at the supplier's cost. A repeated or serious failure triggers a formal written warning with a defined correction period. Persistent failure gives you the right to withhold further orders, to suspend the affected programme, or to terminate without penalty for goods already in production but not yet delivered. Two clauses are worth adding. The first is a right to require a second source to qualify, or to produce a portion of the order, at the supplier's cost where a quality failure causes you to incur a documented qualification expense, which is the clause that makes the risk economically real to a supplier who otherwise has no reason to prioritise your programme. The second is a right to inspect, return or destroy non-conforming material that was shipped before the failure was detected, at the supplier's cost, so that defective ribbon does not enter your own assembly operation and become your problem rather than theirs.

What a good SQA produces in practice

The point of all of this is not the document itself, it is what the document makes possible. When the SQA exists, the sample approval meeting produces a record rather than a memory, the inspection result is a decision rather than a discussion, the supplier change is a conversation you are entitled to have rather than a discovery, and the CAPA conversation is about the process rather than about blame. Buyers who have one consistently report three things: fewer disputes, faster reorder cycles because the knowledge stays inside the relationship, and a supplier who quotes repeat orders more competitively because the specification and the artwork no longer need to be reconstructed every time.

The buyers who do not have one report something different and more predictable: a colour argument in month nine, a packing format change nobody announced, an inspection that fails on a criterion that was never written down, and a CAPA response that consists of an apology and a replacement shipment.

For buyers who are starting from scratch, the practical order is straightforward. Take the most recent specification sheet, the artwork file and the inspection instruction you already have, and write a short agreement that references all three with version numbers and states the eight areas above. Seal the standard sample and give it a number. Agree the defect classification and AQL with the supplier once and write them into the inspection instruction. Draft the CAPA procedure with its five stages and its notification deadline. Define what counts as a material change and set a notice period you would actually be able to honour. Put a review date in the calendar and treat it as a meeting. It is a day of work, and on a programme that will run for years it isthe cheapest insurance available to a custom ribbon programme.

FAQ — Custom ribbon supplier quality agreement

What is a supplier quality agreement for custom ribbon, and why do I need one?

A supplier quality agreement is the document that sits between the purchase order and the quality system. The purchase order tells the supplier what to make, when and how many; the quality agreement tells both parties how quality will be defined, measured, documented and escalated when it is not met. In custom ribbon the two are not enough on their own, because almost every attribute of the goods is a one-off: a new print design, a new colour match, a new construction and a new packing format, none of which the supplier has produced before and none of which the order can describe in a line and a quantity. Without a written quality agreement those attributes get agreed verbally at sample approval, which is exactly where they get misremembered. The document is also what makes your inspection programme enforceable. An AQL, a defect classification and a sealed standard sample are only useful if both sides have agreed in advance that they are the reference, and that agreement is what the quality agreement records. In practice it removes three recurring sources of argument: whether the colour you approved is the colour you received, whether the width tolerance applies before or after finishing, and what happens when a lot fails. It is also what a serious supplier expects, because a well-drafted agreement protects them as much as it protects you. It replaces six email threads with one document that both sides can point at.

What clauses should a ribbon supplier quality agreement contain?

A workable agreement has eight sections. First, scope and applicability, defining which products, constructions and order types it covers and when it takes effect. Second, the technical specification reference, naming the controlled documents the order is built on, typically your specification sheet, the approved artwork or print file, the colour standard and the sealed physical sample, each with a version number and a location, because a reference without a version is not a reference. Third, material and construction requirements, covering fibre content, width and tolerance, construction, colourfastness, shrinkage and any applicable test methods. Fourth, decoration requirements, covering print method, colour count, coverage, registration tolerance, edge treatment, wire specification for wired ribbon, and bow tension and component count where relevant. Fifth, packaging and labelling, covering reel and carton format, labelling content, barcode requirements and pallet specification. Sixth, quality control and acceptance, referencing your inspection instruction, the defect classification, the AQL and the sampling plan. Seventh, non-conforming material, defining how defects are reported, contained, dispositioned and who carries the cost of rework, replacement and reinspection. Eighth, governance, covering audit rights, change notification, documentation retention, and the escalation path. The order of these sections matters less than the fact that each has an owner and a document behind it. A quality agreement that is a list of aspirations with no referenced documents will not survive contact with a production rush.

How do I define acceptable quality and defect tolerance for a custom ribbon order?

The tolerance in a ribbon quality agreement has to be specific enough to be measured and general enough to be applied by anyone. That means numbers, not adjectives. Width, for example, should be stated as a nominal value with a plus or minus tolerance measured at a stated number of positions across the reel, not as "consistent width". Colour should be stated as an acceptable delta E range measured under a stated light source against the sealed standard sample, or as an agreed comparison method if the buyer does not have instrumentation, and should explicitly recognise normal dye-lot variation within the stated limit so that legitimate variation is not treated as a defect. Print should carry a registration tolerance in millimetres, a minimum ink coverage or minimum ink-to-substrate ratio for areas where rubbing and colour loss matter, and a bleed-through limit for narrow or light constructions. Shrinkage after wash or heat exposure should be stated as a percentage if the ribbon will be washed, and it should be stated even for ribbons that will not be washed, because the figure affects cut planning downstream. Elastic recovery and bow pull strength are worth specifying where the ribbon is used in bows or elastic applications. And the agreement should state how defects are classified into critical, major and minor, and link that classification to the AQL and the sampling plan so that the acceptance criteria and the inspection method are not two different documents describing two different ideas.

What is CAPA and how should it be written into a ribbon quality agreement?

CAPA means corrective and preventive action, and it is the clause that separates a quality agreement from a list of specifications. Specifications tell the supplier what the goods should be. CAPA tells both parties what happens when they are not, and what must change so that it does not happen again. A ribbon quality agreement should define a CAPA procedure with a clear structure. First, containment: the defective quantity is identified, quarantined and prevented from shipping, and the buyer is notified within a stated time, typically within twenty-four hours of discovery. Second, root cause analysis, where the supplier is required to investigate rather than simply replace, using a recognised method such as five whys, fishbone analysis or a process FMEA, and to report the analysis in writing. Third, corrective action, describing the specific change made to the process, machine, method, material or training that will prevent recurrence. Fourth, verification of effectiveness, which requires evidence that the corrective action actually worked, typically by a further passing inspection or a defined number of clean lots. Fifth, preventive action, where the same root cause could affect other products, orders or constructions, which is the part most agreements omit and the part that turns a single-order fix into a supplier improvement. The agreement should also state a closure deadline, specify what evidence is required to close the action, and make clear that CAPA closure is a condition of continued business rather than an optional courtesy.

How should traceability and change control be handled in a ribbon supply agreement?

Traceability and change control are the two clauses that protect you when a problem appears several months after the goods were consumed, and they are the two most frequently omitted. Traceability means that any finished carton can be tied back to the fibre source, the dye lot, the print plate or screen, the production shift, the finishing line and the QC records, which means the supplier must be able to demonstrate that capability in a format you can read. For ribbon this is not theoretical, because a shade variation that appears in one shipment and not another usually comes from a different dye lot, and without dye-lot traceability you cannot prove it. Change control means that the supplier must not change anything material without written notice and your written approval, and the notice period should be long enough to be meaningful, typically sixty to ninety days for a change to fibre source, dye house, print line, finishing route, machinery or sub-supplier. Notice periods of one week are useless because by then the goods are already in production. It is also worth defining what counts as a material change in the first place, because without a definition the clause gets argued: a change of thread supplier, a change of print cylinder supplier, a change of adhesive in the tipping, a change of winding machine and a change of carton supplier all sound minor and none of them are. Put the list in the agreement so the question does not arise.

What audit rights should I include, and how are they different from a factory visit?

A factory visit and an audit are different instruments, and the agreement should provide for both. A visit is an opportunity to see the operation, talk to people and build a relationship, and it is best used to understand how the supplier actually works, which is often different from how they describe it. An audit is a formal assessment against a written standard, producing a report, findings, corrective actions and a closure date, and it needs to be scheduled, scoped and documented. The clause should specify the notice period for each type, which can be short and informal for visits and longer and formal for audits, the frequency, the scope, who may attend, whether you may bring your own inspector or use a third-party agency, and what the supplier must make available during the audit, including process records, in-process inspection records, calibration records, raw material certificates, test reports and non-conforming material records. It should also state that the supplier may not refuse an audit on the grounds of customer confidentiality in a way that prevents inspection of the production areas relevant to your goods, and it should give you the right to audit sub-suppliers where a significant part of the product is bought in. The practical value of the audit right is mostly deterrent: a supplier who knows the audit can be scheduled and used to exclude a sub-supplier behaves differently in the ordinary course.

How long should a ribbon supplier quality agreement last, and how often should it be reviewed?

Treat it as a living document with a formal annual review rather than a signed-and-filed contract. An initial agreement normally runs for one to three years, with the specification and artwork references updated by revision rather than by rewriting, so that the history stays intact and you can always see which version applied to which order. Review it at least annually, and immediately whenever something happens that the agreement did not anticipate, which in practice means after every significant CAPA, after every change in construction, print method or market, and after any change in your own product specification. Make the review a scheduled meeting rather than a document refresh, because the value of the review is in the conversation about what failed and what to change, not in re-reading clauses that have not changed. Include in the review the drift in defect rates, the number and age of open CAPAs, the audit findings from the last cycle, any change in sub-suppliers, the current dye-lot range for your standard colours, and whether the AQL and inspection level are still matched to the commercial value of the goods. An agreement that has never been reviewed after two years is worse than no agreement, because it creates the false impression of a controlled relationship while the actual practice has moved on.

What happens if the supplier does not follow the quality agreement?

The remedy structure should be defined in advance so that enforcement does not require a confrontation at the moment the problem is worst. A typical ladder starts with containment and CAPA for an isolated incident, moves to enhanced monitoring and additional inspection at the cost of the supplier where the failure rate exceeds an agreed threshold over a rolling period, then to a formal written warning with a defined correction period, and finally to a right to withhold further orders, to suspend the affected programme or to terminate without penalty for outstanding but undelivered goods. Add two clauses that matter more than they appear to. The first is the right to require a second source to qualify or to produce a portion of the order at the cost of the supplier where a quality failure causes the buyer to incur a documented qualification expense. The second is the right to inspect and take back or destroy non-conforming material that was shipped before the failure was detected, including the cost of return, so that defective ribbon does not enter your own assembly operation. Keep the escalation proportionate and let the parties record any agreed departure from it, because an agreement with no room for mutual accommodation gets followed exactly in the moment when following it exactly is most expensive to both sides.
