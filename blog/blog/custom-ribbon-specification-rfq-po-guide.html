How to Write a Custom Ribbon Specification: An RFQ-to-PO Guide for OEM Buyers

Every outcome in a custom ribbon programme — the price you pay, the sample you approve, the date the goods arrive, whether the carton passes inspection — traces back to one document: the specification. A specification that is loose, partial or written in marketing language produces a quotation that cannot be compared, a sample that surprises you, and a purchase order that contains arguments instead of commitments. A specification that is precise does the opposite: it makes quotations comparable, makes approvals meaningful, and makes the purchase order a short administrative step instead of a second negotiation.

Published 30 October 2026 · 14 min read · Xiamen Meisida Decoration Co., Ltd.

This guide is written from the factory side of the table. We manufacture custom ribbon, printed ribbon and hand-tied bows in Xiamen since 2007, and we quote for brand buyers, retail procurement teams and importers in more than 80 countries. Almost every quotation dispute we are asked to resolve traces back to something that was never written down, or was written down in a way that allowed two readings. This is the guide we would want a buyer to have in front of them before they send the first request for quotation.

Why the specification decides everything downstream

Ribbon buying has an unusual characteristic that catches new buyers out. A ribbon is a narrow textile with a very large number of independent variables, and the variables multiply rather than add. Width, fibre, construction, colour, decoration method, colour count, repeat length, finish, cut length, selvedge treatment, folding, tipping, packaging and labelling are each independently variable, and each combination can be quoted differently.

This produces a familiar outcome. Two buyers send two requests for quotation that both read "custom printed satin ribbon, red, 20mm, 5,000 metres for gift packaging". One buyer receives a quote at USD 0.18 per metre, the other receives USD 0.47 per metre. Neither is wrong, and neither quote is comparable, because the two requests did not describe the same product. The first buyer specified a single-colour screen print on a 100% polyester satin; the second buyer specified a full-coverage multi-colour print with a woven selvedge and a branded foil logo.

Nothing went wrong in either case. The buyers simply asked a question that does not have a single answer. The specification's job is to make the question answerable.

What a specification is, and what it is not

A specification is a complete, unambiguous description of one physical product, written so that a supplier who has never seen your brand can price it and build it without asking a single follow-up question. That last clause is the acid test. If a competent supplier must email you to ask what you mean, the specification has a gap.

What a specification is not: a brand description, a product brief, a mood board, a link to a competitor's product, or a phrase such as "like our previous season but brighter". These are inputs to a specification, not specifications. They are genuinely useful when you are choosing a supplier and you do not yet know the production details, but they cannot be used to price or to build against, and they are not acceptable as the basis of a purchase order.

Three documents, one chain of truth

The documents in a custom ribbon programme are usually three, and they should be one continuous chain rather than three independent pieces of paperwork.

DocumentWhat it doesLegal status
Specification sheetDefines the product: construction, decoration, finishing, packaging, tolerancesA technical reference; no obligation to buy
RFQ (request for quotation)Asks a supplier to price the specification, state lead time, confirm MOQ and propose termsEnquiry only; no obligation to buy
Purchase order (PO)Commits to buy a stated quantity at a stated price on stated terms against a stated specificationBinding on both sides

The discipline that makes this work is simple: the specification does not change after the RFQ goes out. If you need to change something, you reissue the specification with a new revision number, you obtain fresh quotations against it, and you re-approve. The alternative — a PO that silently differs from the RFQ and from the approved sample — is the single most common cause of disputes in this industry, and it is always avoidable.

The specification sheet: eight field groups, field by field

A practical ribbon specification has eight groups. Not every programme needs every field in the first revision — a simple solid-colour ribbon genuinely does not — but every field that is relevant must be filled in rather than left blank, because a blank field is an invitation to the supplier to fill it in with their assumption.

Group 1: Product identity and programme reference

Start with the administrative spine. Give the programme a name and a revision number that both parties will use for the life of the project, and reference it on every subsequent document: the RFQ, the sample approval form, the quotation, the purchase order, the packing list and the inspection report.

The fields are straightforward:

  • Programme reference — a code such as PRD-2026-GIFT-014, revision number and date.
  • Item name and number — the SKU your team and your warehouse will use.
  • Intended application — one line stating where the ribbon ends up: gift box wrap, cosmetic bottle neck, gift bag handle, hair accessory, Christmas decoration, pet collar trim, apparel trim.
  • Buyer contact — one named person with authority to approve samples and one named person with authority to place orders.

The intended application is not decoration. It changes technical decisions in ways that are easy to miss. Ribbon on a perfume bottle needs a specific stiffness and a defined loop length. Ribbon on a hair accessory has different pull forces and a different edge-finishing requirement. Ribbon on a Christmas decoration will be handled, folded and stored in a hot warehouse. Telling the supplier the application is often the fastest route to a technically sound specification, because the factory will know what usually goes wrong in that application.

Group 2: Construction

This is the physical ribbon before anything is done to it, and it is the group where under-specification costs the most, because construction drives material cost, hand feel, durability and print compatibility simultaneously.

One point deserves emphasis because it recurs: state colour as a standard reference, not a description. "Deep festive red" means nothing. Pantone 186 C means the dye house has a target, the lab dip has a measurable delta, and the goods-in inspector has something to check against. If you genuinely need a shade that is not a standard Pantone, say so explicitly and budget for a custom dye batch, including its minimum weight and its own lead time.

Group 3: Decoration

This group tells the factory how the design gets onto the ribbon. It is where most buyers under-specify, usually because they assume the method is obvious from the artwork. It is not, because the same artwork can be produced by four methods with very different costs, lead times, tactile results and durability.

  • Method — screen print, heat transfer, sublimation (polyester only), woven jacquard, foil stamping, heat transfer foil, embroidery, no decoration (solid colour).
  • Colour count — number of inks or passes per repeat. For woven jacquard, the number of colours in the pattern.
  • Repeat length — the length over which the pattern repeats, in millimetres. This is the field buyers forget, and it determines how many repeats fit on a 100-metre roll, which determines how much waste your cutting creates.
  • Coverage — the proportion of the ribbon surface the design covers: full coverage, 40%, a 25mm border, a logo at 30mm.
  • Design files — vector preferred (AI, EPS, PDF), with the scale confirmed and the colour mode stated. Provide a 300dpi photographic reference for textured or photographic designs.
  • Print side — one side or both. Double-sided printing roughly doubles the decoration cost.
  • Effect and durability — whether the result must withstand washing, abrasion, or a specific rub test, and whether the print must be reversible or visible through the ribbon.

Design file quality is worth its own paragraph because it is a hard blocker. A raster image at 72dpi scaled to 25mm will not hold detail, and a factory that has to rebuild artwork will either rebuild it and charge you or, worse, produce something that technically matches the file you sent and not the product you pictured. Sending print-ready vector artwork with the scale confirmed is the fastest possible route to an accurate strike-off, and it saves a sampling round.

Group 4: Finishing and conversion

This is the group with the largest financial consequence, and the one most often left out of the specification entirely. The reason is understandable: it feels like logistics rather than product definition. But finishing is what turns a ribbon into the item you actually ship, and it carries real labour cost.

Specify finishing as a numbered list of operations, so there is no ambiguity about what is included:

  1. Cutting — cut to continuous length, or to fixed lengths (state the length in millimetres with tolerance), or to specific pieces.
  2. Edge treatment — heat-sealed, stitched, or raw.
  3. Hemming / tipping — folded and sealed edges; metal or plastic tipping on cut ends, with the tip material and colour stated.
  4. Folding — flat fold, rolled loop, French loop, or a specific folded shape with the fold position measured from the end.
  5. Pre-tying — tied as a bow, tied onto a tail, or a specified knot style. If you want a specific bow, say so and attach a photograph of the finished bow from the correct side.
  6. Attachment — clips, pins, elastic, backing cards, Velcro, adhesive backing.
  7. Accessories — any wire, tag, sticker or insert included.

Note the difference between ordering metres and ordering pieces. If you order 1,000 metres of ribbon, you receive 1,000 metres of ribbon. If you order 1,000 pieces of 20cm cut lengths, folded and hand-tied into bows, somebody has to cut them, fold them and tie them, and those operations carry a labour rate that has nothing to do with the material. The specification must state which product you are buying, because the price per unit is calculated on that basis and the invoice will be issued against it.

Group 5: Packaging and labelling

Packaging is decided here, not at the warehouse. Specify it in the specification, in enough detail that a supplier can price it, and be aware that bespoke packaging design can cost more than the ribbon inside it.

  • Format — spooled on cardboard or plastic cores, wound flat and folded into polybags, or loose folded lengths in cartons.
  • Inner packaging — polybag per colourway, or bulk-packed.
  • Outer packaging — export carton, corrugated, with a specified pieces-per-carton and maximum carton dimensions or weight for your distribution centre.
  • Labelling — barcode format (EAN-13, UPC-A), where the label sits, whether it is on the polybag or the carton, and whether your barcode is supplied by you or generated by the supplier.
  • Carton marks — shipping marks, carton numbering format, and which data you want printed on the carton itself.
  • Compliance labelling — fibre content, country of origin, care instructions, and any warning text your market requires.

The carton dimension question is worth checking against your own distribution centre rather than assuming. A carton that exceeds the manual handling limit of your warehouse, or that cannot be palletised in your standard footprint, generates a cost at the far end that has no relationship to the ribbon.

Group 6: Quality and compliance requirements

This group turns a preference into a standard. Without it, "acceptable quality" is an argument waiting to happen, and it is always the argument that appears after the goods have shipped.

  • Width tolerance — e.g. ±1mm.
  • Length tolerance — e.g. ±1% on nominal metre length, or the standard roll length you require.
  • Colour tolerance — the acceptable Delta E against the approved standard, measured under a stated light source (D65 daylight is the usual reference). Note that a Delta E figure agreed with the factory should specify the measuring instrument, because handheld meters and spectrophotometers do not read identically.
  • Print quality — acceptable registration tolerance between colours, whether minor ink coverage variance is permitted, and the maximum acceptable defect rate.
  • AQL — the acceptable quality limit for the inspection, stated with the sampling standard reference (ISO 2859-1 is the usual convention). State the inspection level and the defect classes.
  • Documentation required — which certificates must accompany the shipment, and which must be available on request before production.
  • Specific market requirements — chemical safety documentation for the destination market, recycled content substantiation where you claim it, and any retailer-specific test requirements.

A word of caution on tolerances. A tolerance that is impossible to hit reliably produces a rejection rate that costs you more than the defect it prevents. It is better to specify the tolerances that genuinely reflect what your business can absorb, and to agree the inspection method with the factory, than to write a specification so tight that any reasonable supplier will decline to quote against it.

Group 7: Commercial parameters

The specification does not need to contain the price — that is what the quotation is for — but it must contain the parameters the price depends on, or the quotation will not be comparable.

  • Quantity per colourway — and per size, if sizes vary. A total quantity without a breakdown cannot be priced, because the setup is per colour and per specification.
  • Annual or programme volume — where there is a committed forward volume, state it, because it changes the price and the supplier's willingness to hold capacity for you.
  • Delivery target — the date the goods must arrive at your warehouse, and separately the latest date they can be shipped and still make it.
  • Destination and Incoterm — the named port or city and the trade term. Comparing an EXW price with a DDP price is not a comparison.
  • Currency and validity — the currency the quotation should be in and how long it should hold.
  • Payment terms — if you have standard terms, state them now rather than negotiating them after the price is agreed.

Group 8: The approval route

Close the specification by stating how approval works, because "we'll have a look at it" is not a process and it will cost you a week every time it happens.

  • Sample stages required — typically a lab dip for colour, a printed strike-off for the design, and a pre-production sample for construction and finishing.
  • Who approves what — name the person or role for each stage. If colour is approved by a designer and construction by a buyer, say so.
  • Approval deadline — how long you will hold each stage, and what happens if you do not respond. Suppliers plan production windows weeks ahead, and an approval that stalls silently pushes your delivery date without telling anyone.
  • Revision control — how changes are requested and recorded, and that each reissue carries a new revision number and reopens quotation if the change is material.

Turning the specification into an RFQ that gets quoted properly

The RFQ is a packaging exercise. Its job is to get the specification in front of a supplier in a form they can quote from without ambiguity, and to ask the questions that make two quotations comparable.

The RFQ package

  1. The specification sheet, as a standalone document with a revision number and date — not as a paragraph in an email body.
  2. Design files — vector artwork with scale confirmed, plus a PDF preview showing exactly what the design should look like on the ribbon, including the repeat.
  3. Reference images — a photograph of the ribbon if you have one, showing the intended hand feel and drape. This is genuinely useful because "satin" varies between mills.
  4. Quantity schedule — a simple table of colourways and quantities, which is what the supplier will build their production plan from.
  5. Delivery and destination — required date, port, Incoterm.
  6. Compliance list — the certificates you need, so the supplier can confirm at quotation stage whether they can provide them rather than three weeks later.
  7. The questions — see below.

The questions that make quotations comparable

A quotation that only contains a unit price and a lead time is not comparable. Ask for the following, in every case, so that each response is structured and you can lay them side by side:

  • Unit price, broken out by component — material, decoration, finishing, tooling and setup, packaging — so you can see what is actually being charged.
  • Total price per colourway and total order value, including any tooling and sampling charges, which are one-off and should not be amortised into your per-unit comparison.
  • MOQ per colourway and per specification, and what happens if you are below it.
  • Lead time, broken into stages — sample, approval, production, finishing, shipment — with the assumption stated (for example, approval within three business days of the sample arriving).
  • Tooling and setup cost, and how long it remains valid for repeat orders.
  • Sample cost and timeline, including courier, and whether the sample cost is credited against the order.
  • Incoterm and named place, and whether the price includes export documentation.
  • Production capacity statement — how much of your quantity they can run per week, and what their current load looks like in your delivery window.
  • Confirmation of documentation — which certificates they hold and which are current.
  • Payment terms and quotation validity.

Asking all ten of every supplier costs you perhaps forty minutes and saves you weeks. The reason is that a structured response surfaces the differences immediately, and the differences are what you are actually trying to find. One supplier quotes a higher unit price and a shorter lead time with no tooling charge; another quotes lower and needs three weeks of tooling. That comparison is invisible if you only asked for the headline price.

Common RFQ mistakes

  • Embedding the spec in email prose. Documents get lost, and version control disappears. Send a document, refer to it by revision number.
  • Asking for a quote without stating quantity. You will receive the price at the MOQ, which is not the price you will pay.
  • Sending artwork in the wrong scale. A logo that should be 30mm wide supplied at 30cm will be quoted and produced at the wrong size, and it may be caught late.
  • Not asking about capacity. A supplier who cannot make 5,000 metres in your window will quote a price based on a schedule they do not have.
  • Comparing quotations that include different things. Ask whether packaging is included, and to require a line-item breakdown so you can normalise the comparison yourself.

    What to do when quotations still do not match

    Even with a perfect specification, you may get three quotations that appear unrelated. This is normal, and it is a solvable problem, because the differences are structural rather than random.

    Normalise them onto the same basis before you compare:

    1. Confirm each quotation is for the same specification revision. A supplier quoting revision 2 against your revision 3 is not competing with the others.
    2. Separate the one-off costs from the recurring costs. Tooling, sampling, plate and screen charges and artwork setup are one-off; material, decoration, finishing, packaging and freight are recurring.
    3. Add the recurring freight estimate yourself, on the same Incoterm and the same route, so you are comparing landed cost rather than ex-works cost.
    4. Convert to a single unit of comparison — usually cost per finished piece, or cost per metre including an assumed finishing and packing load.
    5. Note the lead time and the capacity statement, and treat them as part of the price. A cheaper quotation that arrives six weeks after you need the goods is not cheaper.

    After normalisation, a large spread usually means one of three things: the suppliers are genuinely quoting different products, the specification left one significant variable open, or one supplier is buying a better position and the other is buying a better price. Only the second is your problem to fix, and the fix is to specify more precisely.

    From the approved sample to the purchase order

    The sample approval is where a specification stops being a description and becomes a commitment. The critical discipline is to convert the physical sample into written, measurable attributes rather than into a feeling of approval.

    What the approved sample must record

    When you sign off a sample, write down the following and attach them to the approval form with the sample reference number:

    • Measured width in millimetres, measured on the approved sample, with the tolerance that will apply to bulk.
    • Construction type — confirmed against what was specified, and whether the sample is the exact base material or a close equivalent.
    • Colour reference — the lab dip or Pantone number, plus the light source under which it was assessed, plus the measured Delta E if one was taken.
    • Colour count and print method — as produced on the sample.
    • Measured repeat length in millimetres and the repeat direction along the ribbon.
    • Print coverage and effect — whether the sample shows the intended ink density and whether the finish matches the brief.
    • Selvedge condition — woven, sealed or cut.
    • Finished dimensions of the converted item — cut length, loop length, bow width and height, and the position of any attachment.
    • Packaging format as packed in the sample.
    • Any accepted deviation, stated explicitly and signed. This is the most valuable line in the whole document, because it converts a conversation into an agreement.

    The rule that protects you

    Never approve a sample verbally, and never approve a sample without writing down what it is. A sample approval with no written attributes is an opinion, and opinions are unenforceable when 20,000 metres of ribbon arrive four months later. The approval form is short — one page, ten fields — and it is the document that carries your intent across an ocean and a quarter of a year.

    How to handle a change after approval

    Changes happen. Artwork is amended, a Pantone is corrected, a quantity moves. The rule is that a change to a specified attribute is a new revision of the specification, it requires re-quotation if it affects cost, and it requires a fresh sample if it affects appearance. The purpose is not bureaucracy; it is that an undocumented change is indistinguishable from a factory error when the goods land, and the argument will be had without you.

    Small changes should be batched rather than made one at a time. A programme with eleven minor amendments generates eleven sample rounds and eleven possible differences between the approval and the bulk. Collecting amendments into a single revised specification, issued once a week, keeps the number of approval cycles down and reduces the number of variables the factory has to track.

    What a ribbon purchase order must contain

    The purchase order is the document that converts an approved product into a commercial obligation. It is short, and its clauses are unremarkable, but two of them are the ones that decide whether a delayed or non-conforming shipment becomes a manageable problem or an expensive one.

    The mandatory content

    ClauseWhat it must say
    Specification referenceProgramme code, revision number and date, plus the approved sample reference
    Item linesEach colourway and size, with the quantity per line and the total
    PricingUnit price and total per line, the tooling and setup charges, and the Incoterm with named place
    Delivery datesThe required arrival date and the latest reasonable ship date, stated separately
    PackagingCarton format, pieces per carton, labelling and barcode requirements
    DocumentationCertificates and test reports required to accompany the shipment

    The two clauses buyers most often omit

    The ship date, stated separately from the delivery date

    A purchase order that says "deliver by 15 November" is ambiguous, because it does not say when the goods must leave. A factory that ships on 14 November and clears customs on 3 December has technically met a 15 November delivery date, while the campaign behind the ribbon launched a fortnight early without it. Specify both dates and state the transit assumption explicitly — for example, 30 days sea freight from Xiamen to the named port, plus a stated allowance for customs clearance. Without that, every late delivery is arguable, and arguable is exactly the state you do not want to be in when a retail programme is waiting.

    The quantity tolerance

    Almost nobody agrees this in advance, and almost everybody argues about it afterwards. Textile production does not yield exact quantities. A run of 5,000 metres per colourway may produce 5,020 or 4,980 depending on the roll, the changeover and the trimming allowance. If the PO is silent, the factory either ships short without explanation or ships over without charging, and in both cases you have a variance that was never agreed and a supplier who is now, reasonably, unsure what you expect next time.

    The standard practice is a tolerance of roughly three to five percent, with overage billed at the agreed unit price and underage either refunded pro rata or the line simply closed. Agree it in advance and put it in writing; it is a small clause that prevents a disproportionate argument.

    Retain the option to cancel one line

    Where a programme has several lines with different deadlines or different documentation requirements, consider splitting the purchase order so that a slow-moving line can be cancelled without affecting the rest. The trade-off is that splitting a programme into multiple orders increases setup costs and reduces your negotiating leverage, so it should be a deliberate decision based on the risk, not a default. Where lines share the same delivery date, same compliance documentation and same destination, keep them together: consolidation is almost always cheaper and much simpler to manage.

    How the specification connects to the rest of the buying process

    A good specification is not an isolated document. It is the upstream end of a chain, and it makes each downstream stage faster and cheaper.

    Quotation becomes comparable

    When every supplier is quoting the same specification, comparison becomes a matter of arithmetic on unit price, lead time and capacity rather than a matter of deciphering what was actually being offered. This is where a well-written specification earns its keep: the time saved in this stage is the time that would otherwise be spent discovering in week six that the two quotations were for different products.

    Sampling becomes targeted

    A precise specification tells the factory exactly which samples to make. The colour approval is a lab dip, the design approval is a strike-off on the actual base material, and the construction and finishing approval is a pre-production sample. Each stage then proves one specific thing rather than testing everything at once. Sampling is the stage where a specification pays for itself most visibly, because each avoided round is a week of calendar time on a retail programme.

    Inspection becomes objective

    An inspector standing in your warehouse with a written specification and a recorded approval can measure width, count metres, check colour against a standard and verify print count. The same inspector without those documents can only form an opinion. Objective inspection means disputes are settled by measurement rather than by argument, and claims are made with evidence.

    Repeat ordering becomes trivial

    This is the commercial benefit that tends to be underestimated. A supplier holding a specification with a revision number, an approval record and a tooling reference can quote a repeat order in a day, at a known price, with no re-sampling and no new setup cost. For a brand with a seasonal or continuous programme, that speed is worth real money, and it is the difference between a supply partner and a series of one-off transactions.

    Ten questions to ask any ribbon supplier before you write the PO

    These are the questions that separate a supplier who can deliver your programme from one who is hoping to learn as they go. Ask all of them, and compare the answers rather than the words.

    1. What is the minimum order quantity per colourway and per specification, and what does it cost to run below it? The answer tells you whether your programme fits their production economics or fights them.
    2. Which part of this programme is your production bottleneck? Weaving, dyeing, printing or finishing. The answer tells you which stage to protect with an early ship date.
    3. What can you produce per week for this specification? A number, not a range. Compare it against your quantity and your window, and do the arithmetic yourself.
    4. How long is the tooling valid, and what happens to the price if I reorder in a later season? Repeat-order pricing is where a long-term relationship actually pays out.
    5. What are the most common reasons your buyers reject ribbon at goods-in? A supplier who answers honestly is telling you what to specify.
    6. Can you hold capacity for my delivery window, and what is your current load in that window? The honest answer is a number. The evasive answer is "we are flexible".
    7. What is your sample lead time, and is the sample cost credited against the order?
    8. Which certifications do you hold, and can you send current copies with the quotation?
    9. What quantity tolerance and lead-time commitment do you put in your purchase orders? A supplier who has a clear answer has a process.
    10. Who is my day-to-day contact, and what is the escalation path when something goes wrong mid-production? This matters far more than it sounds at the point of quotation, and far less than it sounds at the point of crisis.

    Common mistakes that cost buyers the most

    Across the programmes we have quoted and produced, a small number of errors account for most of the avoidable cost and delay. None of them are exotic.

    • Specifying by appearance. "Like last season but brighter" is not a specification. This is the root cause of most sample rejections and most claims.
    • Omitting the finishing. The quoted metre price and the invoiced piece price end up very different, and the discovery happens at invoice stage, when it is least welcome.
    • Not stating quantity per colourway. The supplier prices at their MOQ and you compare it against your real requirement.
    • Asking for a delivery date without a ship date. Late delivery becomes arguable rather than actionable.
    • Not agreeing a quantity tolerance in advance. Every variance becomes a dispute.
    • Approving samples verbally. The approval cannot be enforced, and it cannot be referenced by the inspector.
    • Letting amendments accumulate unrecorded. The bulk no longer matches anything that was ever signed.
    • Treating the specification as a document you file rather than a tool you use. The best specification is a living document that is reopened every time the programme changes.

    A closing note from the factory floor

    There is a particular kind of conversation we have with buyers that always goes the same way. It starts with an order for a ribbon that is hard to describe, moves through a quotation, a sample, a couple of rounds of adjustment, and ends with a delivery that is broadly right but not quite what was imagined. Then, on the next programme, someone writes a proper specification, and the whole cycle compresses dramatically — the quotation comes back in a day, the sample arrives approved on the first round, and the order arrives on the date that was written down nine months earlier.

    That is not the factory being unusually capable. That is what happens when both sides are working from the same written document. The specification is not paperwork that slows the process down; it is the thing that makes the process fast, because it removes every decision that would otherwise have to be made by email, by phone, or by guesswork, often at a point where the cost of getting it wrong has already been incurred.

    Frequently asked questions

    What should a custom ribbon specification include?
    A usable custom ribbon specification needs eight field groups: product identity and programme reference, construction (fibre, width, construction type, colour and Pantone reference, surface finish), decoration method with colour count and repeat length, finishing and conversion (cut lengths, selvedge treatment, tipping, folding, pre-tying, attachment), packaging and labelling, quality and compliance requirements with tolerances and AQL, commercial parameters including quantity per colourway, delivery target and Incoterm, and the approval route naming who signs off on samples. Any spec that omits one of these groups will produce a quotation that is incomplete, because the supplier must price what is not stated and guess at it.
    How detailed should a ribbon RFQ be?
    Detailed enough that three different suppliers quote the same physical product. That means stating widths in millimetres, colours against a Pantone reference rather than a colour name, print method and colour count, quantities per colourway rather than a total, finishing operations as a numbered list, and the delivery port and Incoterm. Vague descriptions such as "nice quality red ribbon for gift boxes" produce quotations that are not comparable and disputes that are settled by whoever argues hardest. Include a reference image and, when the design exists, an AI or vector file with dimensions confirmed.
    What is the difference between an RFQ and a purchase order for ribbon?
    The RFQ is a request for information that asks a supplier to state a price and lead time against a specification, and it carries no obligation to buy. The purchase order is a binding commitment to buy a stated quantity at a stated price on stated terms, and it becomes the master document that the invoice, packing list and shipping documents must all agree with. In practice the RFQ should already contain the full specification, so that approving a quotation and issuing a purchase order is a short step rather than a fresh negotiation. Any change between the approved quotation and the purchase order should be treated as a new approval cycle.
    How do I convert an approved sample into a specification?
    Treat the approved sample as a physical annex to the specification, with a signed reference number, and write down what the sample actually establishes rather than what you assumed it established. Record the measured width in millimetres, the construction type, the Pantone or lab dip reference and the light source used for assessment, the colour count, the measured repeat length in millimetres, the print coverage percentage, the finished length tolerance, the selvedge condition and the packaging format. Then state the acceptable tolerance for each measurable attribute. A sample without a written tolerance record is an opinion, and opinions are not enforceable when the bulk arrives.
    What should be on a ribbon purchase order to protect delivery dates?
    At minimum: the specification reference and the approved sample reference, the itemised quantity per colourway and per size, the unit price and the total value, the Incoterm with named place, the required delivery date and the latest reasonable ship date that still meets it, the packaging specification, the documentation required with the shipment, the payment terms, the tolerance policy for quantity and quality variation, the remedy for late delivery, and the governing law and dispute mechanism. The two clauses buyers most often omit are the ship date as distinct from the delivery date, and a written quantity tolerance, which is where most claims and most goodwill are lost.
    How many ribbon specifications should one purchase order cover?
    Group as many specifications as the supplier can run in a single production window without compromising the delivery date, and separate only what genuinely needs separating. Every additional specification adds a setup, and every additional colourway adds colour-matching and ink costs, so consolidating is normally cheaper and simpler to manage. Separate purchase orders become necessary when a programme has a hard delivery deadline, when a specification requires different compliance documentation, when the pricing basis differs, or when you want to retain the option to cancel one line without affecting the others. The trade-off is simple: one order per programme is cheaper and faster to manage, but it concentrates your risk.
    What is the most common mistake buyers make when specifying ribbon?
    Specifying by appearance rather than by attribute. Descriptions such as "similar to the sample from last year" or "a good quality red" leave the key variables undefined: the fibre, the width, the construction, the actual colour standard, the print method, the colour count and the tolerances. The result is a quote that is not comparable, a sample that differs from expectations, and a bulk delivery that is technically compliant with the specification while being visibly wrong. The second most common mistake is leaving the finishing operations out of the specification, which means the piece price quoted is not the piece price invoiced.
