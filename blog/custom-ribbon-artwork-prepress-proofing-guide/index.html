Custom Ribbon Artwork and Prepress Proofing: A Buyer Guide to Print-Ready Files, Dielines and Strike-Offs

Most ribbon sourcing problems are not solved by finding a better factory or a sharper price. They are solved at the very first stage of the project, before anyone weaves a metre of ribbon: the stage where your brand artwork is converted into a print-ready file that a ribbon line can actually run. A logo that looks perfect on a laptop screen, a dieline drawn to the wrong repeat length, a Pantone reference written into an email instead of into the file — each of these produces a physical roll of ribbon that is wrong in a way that cannot be fixed, and each of them produces the same predictable outcome: a reprint, a delay, a cost, and a relationship that has started with an argument.

This guide is about the artwork and prepress stage of custom ribbon production. It covers the file formats that survive contact with a ribbon factory, the specifications that actually matter for woven, printed and finished ribbon, how repeat length and dieline design determine both cost and quality, how colour proofing and strike-off approval should be structured, and how to build a documentation package that makes reordering trivial. Since 2007, MSD Ribbon has handled artwork intake for brand buyers and retail procurement teams across more than fifty countries, and the patterns described here come from that accumulated experience rather than from theory.

Why ribbon artwork is not like artwork for paper

Brand teams are accustomed to designing for flat, stationary substrates: a printed carton, a paper bag, a label. Those substrates are dimensionally stable, they do not move, they do not stretch, and they do not have a grain direction. Ribbon violates every one of those assumptions, and the gap between a print designer’s mental model and a ribbon line’s physical reality is where most of the avoidable cost lives.

  • The material moves. Ribbon is woven on a loom and then runs continuously through printing, embossing, edge-finishing and winding equipment. Anything that has to line up — a repeated logo, a border, a measurement mark — must survive the fact that the web is under tension and slightly less stable than paper.
  • There is a grain direction. Warp and weft behave differently. A design that relies on perfect isotropy will not behave the way it does on paper, and certain effects such as embossing or foil are directional.
  • The surface is not flat. Satin has a pronounced specular sheen and a surface texture; grosgrain has visible ribs running lengthwise; organza is sheer and open; velvet is soft and light-absorbing. The same artwork file produces a completely different impression on each of these.
  • Detail has an effective floor. Fine detail that prints cleanly on a 300 dpi sheet can fill in, disappear or break up on a woven substrate, particularly at speed and on narrow widths.
  • Colour is decided twice. Once by the file, then again by the substrate and the dye or ink. The base colour of the ribbon, the colour of the print, and the interaction between the two determine the final appearance — and only one of those three is in your artwork file.

The practical consequence is that a ribbon artwork file is not a logo placed in a rectangle. It is a production instruction for a specific width, a specific material, a specific repeat length and a specific decoration method — and it must say so unambiguously.

The four decisions that must be settled before artwork starts

In our experience, projects that go smoothly are the ones where these four decisions are made deliberately, in writing, before a single file is drawn. Everything else in the artwork conversation then becomes detail rather than debate.

1. Material and construction

Material determines what is achievable with artwork, so it comes first even though artwork usually comes first in the buyer's mind. Polyester satin accepts fine detail and a bright sheen. Grosgrain has a textured rib that competes with fine line work and rewards bolder, simpler graphics. Velvet is light-absorbing, so saturated colour reads darker and contrast needs to be higher. Organza and sheer fabrics require artwork with generous spacing because the background shows through. Cotton and linen accept dye-sublimation printing beautifully but cannot be heat-stamped or hot-foiled reliably. Woven-edge, wired, metallic, and reflective constructions each impose their own constraints.

Ask for a physical ribbon sample — not a photograph, not a screen rendering — and place your artwork on it. This is the single most effective prepress step and it costs a few days.

2. Width and edge construction

Width constrains artwork directly. A 10 mm ribbon with a 20 mm-wide logo does not work, and scaling a logo down to fit destroys its proportions. Edge construction matters too: a woven edge (selvedge) adds roughly 2–3 mm of solid material at each side, which is unsuitable for full-bleed designs and changes the usable artwork width. A cut-edge ribbon has no such margin and can carry edge-to-edge artwork, but it must be edge-finished to prevent fraying — usually with a heat-seal or over-lock, which again constrains what reaches the edge.

3. Decoration method

Screen printing, digital printing, foil stamping, embossing, debossing and heat transfer each have their own artwork requirements, and some combinations require two files rather than one. Screen printing needs artwork separated into spot colours. Foil stamping needs a solid, opaque foil layer with no fine detail. Embossing and debossing need a clean die profile. Heat transfer needs artwork prepared for a silicone belt or transfer drum. Decide the decoration method with the factory before the artwork is finalised, because a file prepared for the wrong method may be physically impossible to run.

4. Repeat length

Repeat length is the distance over which your design repeats along the length of the ribbon. It is the single most consequential number in the file, because it determines how many repeats fit on a roll, how many repeats the consumer sees per wrap, and — critically — how much waste the production run generates. A 100 mm repeat on a 100 mm roll length creates a systematic one-repeat waste on every roll. This is covered in detail in our guide to ribbon yield and consumption, but the artwork decision that causes it happens long before anyone measures it.

File formats: what to send and what to avoid

A ribbon factory that accepts artwork in five different formats will not necessarily tell you which one it prefers. Specify it yourself, and send the right file.

Preferred formats

  • Adobe Illustrator (.ai) or vector PDF (.pdf) with fonts outlined — the standard for print-ready artwork. All fonts converted to outlines, all text converted to curves, and the document set to the exact repeat length and ribbon width.
  • Adobe Photoshop (.psd) at high resolution — acceptable for photographic or tonal artwork, provided the resolution is genuinely sufficient for the repeat size at the printing speed.
  • PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4 — the standard exchange formats for print production, useful when your design team and your factory use different applications.

Formats that cause problems

  • Low-resolution raster images — a logo exported as a 500-pixel PNG and stretched to 250 mm prints as mush. Logos and type must be vector.
  • Word, PowerPoint or Google Slides documents — these are not artwork files. They are containers that happen to hold artwork, and they lose colour, resolution and spacing when exported.
  • Screen images or screenshots — including references to colours by RGB hex value, which is meaningless for a physical ribbon.
  • Colour specified in prose — "use our brand blue" or "Pantone 286" written in an email body rather than as a spot colour in the file. A spot swatch in the file is a contract; a sentence is not.
  • Layered or messy working files — hidden layers, stray clipping masks and unused objects generate unpredictable results on a production machine. Deliver a clean, flattened print-ready file.

A useful rule: if the file can be opened and printed correctly on any modern machine without the sender being present to explain it, it is ready. If it needs an explanation, it is not ready — and the explanation will be delivered late, at the moment when a line is waiting.

Colour: from Pantone reference to physical match

Colour is where most reprints are born. Screen colours, Pantone book colours and the colours that actually appear on a ribbon are three different things, and only the third one is what your customer sees in a shop.

The reliable sequence runs like this. First, the ribbon base colour is agreed — either from the mill's standard shade range or from a lab dip produced to a physical reference. Second, the print colour is specified as a spot colour in the artwork file, with the Pantone reference noted for the print ink only. Third, a proof is produced on the actual material and checked against a physical reference under a controlled light source. Fourth, the approved proof becomes the standard against which production is judged.

Two practical points deserve emphasis. First, a Pantone reference describes an ink, not an appearance on a woven substrate. The same ink on satin, on grosgrain and on velvet will read differently because the substrate reflects and absorbs light differently — so a colour that matches perfectly on paper can look dull on satin and wrong on velvet. Second, always keep a physical reference of the approved colour in your own archive, sealed against light. Digital files age, screens are uncalibrated and Pantone books get replaced; a sealed physical swatch does not. Our guide to colour matching with Pantone covers the laboratory side of this in more detail.

Print-on-colour versus print-on-white

Printing on a pre-dyed ribbon base is cheaper and more predictable than printing a background colour on white ribbon, because the base is already uniform. It also constrains artwork: the base colour shows wherever the ink does not. When a design needs a specific background colour or a fine gradient, printing on white base ribbon with an opaque underlay becomes necessary — which adds a second print pass, adds setup cost and adds risk. Decide this at the artwork stage, not after the first strike-off disappoints you.

Repeat length and dieline design

The dieline is the template that shows the designer exactly how one repeat of artwork sits on the ribbon. It is a small piece of documentation with an outsized effect, because it is what stops the artwork designer and the production team from working from two different understandings of the same job.

A proper ribbon dieline should specify the following, and should be attached to the artwork file or issued alongside it:

  • Ribbon width in millimetres, measured across the finished ribbon, not across the uncut web.
  • Repeat length in millimetres, plus the number of repeats per standard roll.
  • Selvedge or cut edge, and the usable artwork width once the edge allowance is deducted.
  • Artwork origin — the position of the design relative to the ribbon edge, so that a design that should be centred is actually centred.
  • Registration reference — a small registration mark that shows the factory whether the web is running true.
  • Direction indication — an arrow showing the print direction along the web, since ribbons are frequently wound or converted in ways that flip them.
  • Minimum detail limits — the thinnest line and smallest text the factory can hold on that material and method, expressed in millimetres.
  • Colour bar — a colour reference strip printed in the production colours, used to check ink density and registration on the run.

When a dieline is missing, disagreements about placement, orientation and scale are almost guaranteed, and they surface at the most expensive possible moment — after a production run has already been printed. At MSD Ribbon we issue a dieline for every new artwork as standard, and it is routinely the document that resolves a question in a single reply.

Proofing: strike-off, lab dip and production sample

Three different things are often called a "sample", and confusing them causes a great deal of wasted time and money. It is worth being precise about which one you are working with at each stage.

  • A digital proof is a rendered simulation on screen or on paper. It confirms layout, scale, orientation and spacing. It confirms nothing about colour, texture, hand-feel or how the print sits on the weave. It is a layout check.
  • A lab dip or strike-off is a short physical run of the actual material, printed with the actual inks, in the actual width. This is the colour decision point. Approve the strike-off in writing, referencing it by a version number.
  • A production sample is a section of ribbon taken from the finished run, before or during packing. It is the confirmation that the whole system worked — material, print, finishing, packing — and it is your reference for what future orders should look like.

Two rules govern this stage. First, approve once, in writing, with a clear deadline — "approved subject to nothing" is what protects both parties. Second, version your approvals. Label every proof with a neutral code that does not contain a year (for example PRO-V03), record what changed between versions, and never approve a physical sample that has been left unlabelled on a desk for two weeks, because by then nobody can be certain which version it is.

Because colour approval is the most common source of reprints, agree in advance how approval will be recorded: email is acceptable if it is unambiguous, and a signed approval sheet is better for anything with a high unit cost or a tight launch date. Minimum detail and the limits of a woven surface

Fine detail on ribbon is limited by physics as much as by equipment. A printed line on a woven substrate is broken by the crowns and valleys of the weave, and at production speed the registration tolerance is measured in fractions of a millimetre. As a working rule for planning artwork, keep the thinnest line at or above 0.5 mm, the smallest text at or above 4 mm cap height, and clear space between elements at or above 1 mm. On textured substrates such as grosgrain or velvet, increase all three.

Small solid elements also behave differently from text and fine lines. A solid block of colour spreads slightly as the ink penetrates the weave, so an element that measures 0.6 mm on screen may arrive at 0.9 mm. Conversely, a tight hairline can vanish entirely. Where a design has both, adjust the file to compensate for the substrate rather than expecting the line to come out as drawn.

Metallic, reflective and specialty finishes in artwork

Foils, metallics and reflective yarns introduce a technical layer into artwork preparation that is worth understanding before the file is created.

  • Hot foil stamping requires artwork to be a solid, opaque shape. Fine detail, small text and transparency all cause problems, because the foil does not fill gaps reliably. Prepare foil artwork as a separate spot layer with a defined solid, and set a minimum feature size that your factory confirms for the specific foil and substrate combination.
  • Metallic-effect yarns — Lurex, mylar or film yarn — are integrated into the weave rather than applied on top. Artwork must account for the yarn layout, and the repeat length must be compatible with the yarn course spacing. This is a case where the material, not the design, sets the constraint.
  • Reflective and fluorescent yarns change how the product reads under retail lighting and in photography. If the item will be photographed or filmed for e-commerce, ask for a sample in the final format rather than judging a loose swatch.
  • Iridescent and pearlescent effects shift colour with viewing angle, which makes flat artwork previews misleading and makes a physical sample essential before sign-off.

Build a re-orderable documentation package

The most valuable thing a ribbon buyer can build is not a good price — it is the ability to reorder exactly what they received, two years later, from a different person, without a single email thread. This is achieved with a small, disciplined documentation set, and it is worth assembling at the same time as the artwork.

  • Print-ready artwork file with fonts outlined, in a folder labelled by job and version.
  • The dieline for that exact width and repeat.
  • The approved proof reference, photographed and signed off, with the approval date and the approver's name.
  • A physical swatch of the approved colour, sealed in a labelled bag, stored away from light and heat.
  • The specification sheet — material, construction, width, repeat, print method, colours with their spot references, edge treatment, packing format and test requirements.
  • Tooling ownership terms — who owns the screen, plate or cutting die, and what happens to it if the relationship ends. This belongs in the purchase order or the supply agreement, but it should be decided consciously.

The payoff is disproportionate. When a buyer returns two seasons later for a repeat order, the factory can quote from a specification rather than from a memory, the artwork does not need to be redrawn, and the strike-off that was approved in year one is still the standard. Buyers who maintain this package typically quote reorders weeks faster and with far less margin for error — and they can move production to a second supplier without restarting from zero, because the specification is complete enough for anyone to build to.

Common artwork mistakes that cost money

Across our projects, the same handful of mistakes account for most reprint incidents. None of them are exotic.

  1. Logo supplied as a low-resolution image. Always request vector artwork — EPS, AI or vector PDF. If a raster image is all that exists, ask for the source file rather than a higher-resolution export; there is no digital version of a logo that is better than the vector original.
  2. Colour referenced in the body of an email. Put it in the file as a spot swatch and note the physical reference separately.
  3. Repeat length that does not divide evenly into the roll length. This guarantees a waste tail on every roll. Check the arithmetic before the file is released.
  4. Artwork extending into the selvedge. Full-bleed designs on a woven-edge ribbon will be clipped and will look broken on both edges.
  5. Fine detail below the method's minimum. A hairline in a screen file will not survive the process.
  6. No dieline. Two teams, two assumptions, one argument, one delay.
  7. Approving a proof without version labelling. The version you approved is no longer identifiable two weeks later.
  8. Files delivered without the specification sheet. The artwork answers "what does it look like"; the specification answers "what is it". Both are needed.

Each of these is inexpensive to avoid and expensive to discover after a production run.

How to run a smooth artwork handover: a checklist

The following sequence is the one we recommend to brand buyers and procurement teams. It takes perhaps two hours of focused work and removes most of the friction from the pre-production stage.

  1. Select the material first and request a physical sample of the intended construction.
  2. Confirm width and edge construction, then obtain the dieline from the factory.
  3. Confirm the decoration method and any minimum feature sizes it imposes.
  4. Set the repeat length as a function of the roll length and the intended use on the finished product.
  5. Deliver one vector file, fonts outlined, text converted to curves, spot colours named and defined.
  6. Place the artwork against the dieline and check position, scale, orientation and edge clearance yourself.
  7. Approve a digital proof for layout only, and say so explicitly.
  8. Approve a physical strike-off for colour, and label and record it.
  9. File the documentation package — artwork, dieline, approval record, physical swatch, specification.
  10. State in the purchase order that production is to match the approved strike-off by version number. That single sentence closes the gap between what you approved and what you receive.

Artwork is the cheapest part of a ribbon programme and the part with the widest effect on cost, quality and lead time. It is also the stage that most often gets compressed when a launch date moves in, and compression at this stage is precisely what produces the expensive problems later. At MSD Ribbon we have built our artwork and proofing process around the belief that a well-prepared file is the cheapest quality control a buyer can buy.

Frequently asked questions about custom ribbon artwork

What file format should I send for custom printed ribbon artwork?

Send a print-ready vector file — Adobe Illustrator (.ai) or a vector PDF with all fonts outlined and all text converted to curves. PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4 is the safest interchange format. High-resolution Photoshop (.psd) is acceptable for photographic artwork only. Never send Word, PowerPoint, slides or low-resolution PNG logos for a ribbon production run.

How should I specify colour so that the printed ribbon matches my brand?

Define the colour as a named spot swatch inside the artwork file, note the Pantone reference for the print ink, and approve a physical strike-off on the actual ribbon material under controlled lighting. A Pantone value describes an ink, not its appearance on a woven substrate, so the same ink reads differently on satin, grosgrain and velvet. Keep a sealed physical swatch of the approved colour in your archive for reorders.

What is a ribbon dieline and should I be asking my supplier for one?

A dieline is a scaled template showing the artwork position within the repeat, the ribbon width, the selvedge or cut edge, the artwork origin, the print direction and minimum detail limits. Yes — ask for one for every new artwork. It is the document that prevents the two teams working from different assumptions, and it is frequently what resolves an argument in a single reply rather than a week of emails.

What is the smallest text or line that can be printed on ribbon?

As a working rule, keep the thinnest line at or above 0.5 mm and the smallest text at or above 4 mm cap height, with at least 1 mm of clear space between elements. On textured substrates such as grosgrain, velvet or burlap, increase all three figures. These are planning guidelines for artwork — your factory should confirm the limits for the specific material and print method before the file is finalised.

How do I choose the repeat length for my custom ribbon?

Choose it from two directions at once: how many repeats should the consumer see per wrap of the finished product, and how does the repeat divide into your standard roll length. A repeat that does not divide evenly into the roll creates a waste tail on every roll. Also confirm that the repeat is compatible with woven-effect or metallic yarns if you are using them, since yarn course spacing constrains the repeat independently of your design.

Can the same artwork file be used for printing and for hot foil stamping?

Usually not directly, and it is better to prepare two files. Foil artwork must be a solid, opaque shape with no transparency and with a minimum feature size your factory confirms for the foil and substrate combination. A print-ready file with gradients, fine lines or overlapping elements may be perfectly acceptable for ink printing but physically impossible to run in foil, so decide the decoration method before the artwork is finalised rather than after the first strike-off.

What should I keep on file so that a reorder is fast and accurate?

Keep five things: the print-ready vector artwork file with fonts outlined, the dieline for that width and repeat, a written and versioned record of the approved proof with the approver and date, a sealed physical swatch of the approved colour, and a complete specification sheet covering material, construction, width, repeat, print method, spot colours, edge treatment, packing format and test requirements. With this package, a reorder can be quoted from the specification rather than from a memory, and the second run can be matched to the first without a fresh strike-off.
