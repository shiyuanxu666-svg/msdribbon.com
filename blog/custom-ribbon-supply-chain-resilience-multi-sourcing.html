Custom Ribbon Supply Chain Resilience: Building a Multi-Sourcing Strategy That Survives Disruption

Every sourcing plan is written on the assumption that things go according to schedule. Somewhere in the chain, a dye house has a backlog, a port is closed, a yarn supplier misses a delivery, a power interruption stops a line for two days, or a factory simply declines to accept your order because it is already full in your peak month. None of these are exotic events, and in a business where a single trim can make or break a gift box, a carton of ribbon, a hair accessory line or a festive collection, an interruption of a few weeks is not an inconvenience. It is a cancelled launch. The question that separates a resilient trim supply chain from a fragile one is not whether you have a second supplier on file. It is whether you have tested whether that second supplier could actually take over your programme, and how quickly, at what cost, and with what quality.

This guide works through how to build that resilience for custom ribbon specifically: how to structure a dual-source arrangement that a manufacturer will genuinely accept rather than politely decline, how to split minimum order quantities across suppliers without destroying the unit economics, how to score and rank supplier risk using variables that actually predict disruption, how to write a contingency plan that can be executed in days rather than weeks, and how to protect quality consistency when the same brief is running in two places at once. The intended reader is a procurement manager, supply chain lead, brand owner or retail buyer responsible for a made-to-order trim programme where a stock-out is measured in lost shelf space rather than lost margin on a single order.

Why a single ribbon supplier is a structural risk, not a preference

The usual reason buyers consolidate with one supplier is entirely rational in isolation. A second supplier means a second specification, a second sample approval, a second colour standard, a second set of certificates to verify and a second set of conversations with someone who does not know the history of the programme. The overhead is real, and for a buyer running a small trim budget it can look like an uneconomic luxury.

The problem is that a single-source arrangement for custom ribbon concentrates every category of risk in one point, and several of those categories are outside your control. A custom ribbon programme depends on upstream inputs that the factory buys, not you: the yarn or film stock, the dye lot, the printing subcontractor, the edge finishing, the packaging component supplier. If any one of those fails, your supplier cannot deliver, and your supplier's inability to deliver becomes your inability to deliver. You are exposed to a chain you have never audited and cannot see.

The maths of single sourcing. Consider a typical festive programme where your ribbon represents a modest percentage of unit cost but a very high percentage of unit appeal. If the ribbon is late by four weeks, you do not simply sell the item four weeks later. You lose the entire retail window, because the item is not shipped in time for the season in which it exists. The cost of the disruption is therefore not a function of the ribbon's share of BOM cost. It is a function of the ribbon's share of the product's reason to exist. This is why trim supply chains deserve a disproportionate share of procurement attention.

The concentration problem. A single ribbon supplier also concentrates negotiating power in a way that shows up at the worst possible moment. If your supplier knows that they are your only source for a construction, they know that a price dispute is really a supply threat, and they can hold the line. The existence of a credible alternative, even one used for only twenty percent of the volume, changes that dynamic permanently, and it changes it without any adversarial intent on either side.

The learning argument. A second supplier is also the cheapest insurance policy available in sourcing, because it is the only mechanism that continuously tests whether your primary supplier's claims hold. Once you have a working second source, every conversation you have with your primary about capacity, cost and lead time is anchored in a fact rather than an assertion.

What a genuinely resilient custom ribbon supply chain looks like

Resilience in trim sourcing is not binary. It is a ladder, and most programmes need to be somewhere specific on it rather than at either extreme. The four stages below describe how leading buyers actually deploy capacity, from the minimum viable arrangement upwards.

Stage one: a qualified backup that has never produced for you. This is the baseline that almost every buyer should hold. It means one additional supplier has been through the same evaluation process as your primary, has produced samples against your actual specification, and has those samples approved and physically held in your warehouse. The weakness is obvious and should be understood: the backup has never run a full production order, so the first time you call on it, it will be slower and less certain than the primary. Its value is real, but its purpose is to prevent a total stoppage rather than to preserve the schedule precisely.

Stage two: a dual-source split on a live programme. Here you divide a proportion of the volume, typically twenty to forty percent, between a primary and a secondary who are both producing from the same specification. This is the arrangement most serious buyers aim for, because both suppliers are continuously rehearsing the programme and both carry live tooling and live colour references. The cost is that you are running two production schedules, two inspection regimes and two sets of deliveries, and you need enough volume in each lane to justify the setup cost of the second.

Stage three: a qualified backup plus buffer inventory of the critical construction. This is the version that actually protects a seasonal launch. Because custom ribbon can take ten to sixteen weeks end to end, a buffer is the only strategy that removes the supplier's lead time from the critical path entirely. The buffer does not need to be large. It needs to cover the window between detecting a disruption and getting a replacement order into production, which in practice means four to eight weeks of demand for the most critical colourway or construction.

Stage four: a geographically and technically diversified supply base. This stage applies to high-volume or high-criticality programmes, where you deliberately run different constructions or different production routes in different regions so that no single event can affect the whole. It is more expensive to set up and it demands tighter specification control, because you are now managing variation between suppliers rather than within one.

Choosing your stage. The right answer is driven by three variables: the value at risk if the programme stops, the cost of carrying a second source, and the number of decision-makers you have to manage. A single-product gift brand with a December launch and a narrow season has a high value at risk and should invest in stage three. A steady replenishment programme for a hair accessory line sold year round should sit comfortably at stage two. A business adding trims to a broader catalogue should start at stage one and upgrade as the volume justifies it.

How to qualify a second ribbon supplier properly

The failure mode in dual sourcing is well documented. A buyer contacts a second factory, receives a convincing quotation and a good-looking sample, believes the risk is now covered, and discovers at the moment of crisis that the second supplier has never woven your construction, does not own the dye house, subcontracts the printing, and cannot meet your volume at your width. The qualification has to be done properly, and the work is a fraction of what it seems because much of it can be done in parallel with your primary relationship.

Test the construction, not just the product. The single most important question is whether the second supplier produces your construction in-house. The trade vocabulary obscures this badly. A factory that weaves narrow-width polyester and satin ribbon on its own looms cannot necessarily produce a 38 mm grosgrain with a specific edge finish, because the loom width and the edge-finishing equipment differ. A factory that weaves but outsources heat-transfer printing to a local print house has a dependency you must now verify separately. Ask directly which processes run under the factory's own roof and which are subcontracted, then go and verify the subcontracted steps with the same seriousness you would apply to a primary supplier.

Test the width and the volume together. A supplier may be perfectly capable of weaving your width in a laboratory quantity and completely unable to do it commercially at your monthly volume. The constraint is often the loom, not the skill: a narrow loom cannot be run at a given width and a given tension simultaneously. Require a statement of the production width range and the sustainable monthly volume per width, and ask to see the loom in operation at a width comparable to yours.

Test the colour route. Whether the second supplier dyes in-house or uses an external dye house determines both the control you have over shade consistency and the number of weeks you should add to any switch. An in-house dye house gives you tighter loop control because the lab dip and the production dip are handled by the same team against the same standard. An external dye house is workable, but you should visit it, and you should ask what happens to your shade standard if that dye house changes. Confirm the minimum dye lot quantity, because a small lot is both a cost driver and a schedule constraint.

Test the print and decoration route. Screen printing, digital printing, heat-transfer and woven printing each have different economics and different tolerance behaviour at scale. A supplier with only screen printing cannot take a job requiring short-run digital printing, and vice versa. If your programme uses foil, heat-sealing, wired edges, elastic cores or multi-colour registered printing, verify that the specific decoration capability exists and that it runs on equipment appropriate to your order size.

Run a production trial, not a sample. A sample proves the construction. A small production order, in the low hundreds of metres or a few hundred units, proves the process. This is the step most buyers skip and it is the step that most reliably exposes the difference between a factory that can make a striking sample and a factory that can make your order repeatably. A production trial should be against your real specification, at your real quantity, packed in your real packaging, and inspected against your standard. Budget for it as insurance rather than as waste.

Verify the paperwork in parallel. Certification status, business licence, export history and quality records should be collected at the same time as the samples, not as an afterthought when you are already panicking. A backup supplier without a verified certificate is not a backup; it is a different risk. Ask for the certificates that apply to the fibre and the decoration, verify their validity directly with the issuing body, and confirm that they cover the specific products and colourways you intend to place with them.

Splitting MOQ across two suppliers without wrecking your economics

Minimum order quantity is the structural obstacle to dual sourcing in trims, and it is a real one. Custom ribbon MOQs typically sit between 500 and 5000 metres depending on construction, width, decoration and colour, and printing generally carries a higher minimum than solid colour. If your monthly consumption is 1200 metres, splitting the order means neither supplier is efficient, and the cost of that inefficiency can easily exceed the value of the resilience.

The solution is arithmetic rather than goodwill. Work through the following sequence before deciding how to split.

Calculate your true consumption rate. Not your order size but your actual usage, including yield loss, sampling consumption, cutting waste and safety stock. Ribbon yield in cutting operations is genuinely significant for complex shapes, and many buyers underestimate it substantially. The consumption figure is what tells you whether splitting is even possible.

Identify the split point in the SKU hierarchy. You do not need to split every colourway. The right approach is to rank your ribbon programme by criticality and split only the top tier. A programme with twelve colourways should normally dual-source the two or three that carry the most revenue or the most launch risk, and single-source the remainder. This concentrates the resilience where it matters and avoids the fixed cost of a second setup across every SKU.

Test whether the volume can support two minimums. The arithmetic is straightforward: if your monthly usage is 1200 metres and each supplier's minimum is 1000 metres, a 60/40 split is not available, because neither supplier can take a compliant order. The options are to raise total volume, to negotiate the minimum down for the secondary lane in exchange for a longer commitment, or to accept a smaller split and manage the replenishment cycle accordingly.

Negotiate the minimum, not the price. In practice, secondary suppliers are far more willing to reduce a minimum order quantity than to reduce a price, because a lower minimum changes their economics in a way a discount does not. The trade you can offer is commitment: a longer agreed programme, a forecast you honour, or a smaller first trial in exchange for a lower minimum on the standing order. This is the single most effective lever for making dual sourcing workable in trims, and it costs the buyer nothing that a single-source arrangement was not already consuming in risk.

Account for the colour standard problem. Two suppliers will not produce an identical shade from the same specification, because dye lots and finishing routes differ. This is the hidden cost of dual sourcing in colour-matched trims, and it has to be managed explicitly. The standard is a physical sealed swatch, held by both parties, with a defined tolerance expressed in Delta E and a requirement to submit a strike-off against it for every new dye lot. If your programme is shade-critical across a wide colour range, ask whether a single colourway can realistically be held to a tighter standard by one supplier while the breadth of the range is covered by another.

Watch the tooling and setup economics. Splitting a programme means paying for printing setup twice, or for two suppliers to each hold a screen or plate for your design. That is a real cost and it belongs in the business case. It is also the reason split volumes should be large enough that the setup cost is amortised, which is another argument for splitting fewer SKUs at meaningful volume rather than many SKUs at trivial volume.

Scoring supplier risk using variables that predict disruption

Most supplier risk frameworks used in general manufacturing do not transfer cleanly to trim, because trims are lower value, higher colour sensitivity and more dependent on upstream textile inputs than, for example, a moulded plastic component. A useful scorecard for custom ribbon should weight the things that actually fail. The following six dimensions, weighted as shown, reflect how disruption has actually occurred in this industry.

Upstream input dependency (weight 25%). How much of the critical path passes through materials the supplier does not control? A factory that buys its own yarn and has two qualified yarn suppliers is materially more resilient than one that buys a single speciality yarn from one mill. Ask which inputs are single-sourced, whether the mill relationship is exclusive, and what the lead time is on a replenishment of that specific input. This is the dimension most likely to determine whether a disruption reaches you.

Process ownership (weight 20%). What proportion of the production route runs in-house? A fully integrated factory that weaves, dyes, prints and finishes has fewer handoffs, fewer opportunities for quality drift and fewer third parties to fail. Record the percentage of the route under the factory's own roof, and treat any critical step that is outsourced as a separate risk requiring its own verification.

Capacity headroom (weight 20%). Does the factory have unused capacity in the months you need it, or is it running at full book? Ask for the current utilisation rate by month for the relevant production lines, and for the committed order book in your peak months. A supplier with 40% headroom in December is a different proposition from one at 95%, regardless of how good their samples are. Headroom is also the number that determines how fast they could absorb a volume transfer from a failed supplier, which is the entire purpose of the backup.

Financial and entity stability (weight 15%). How long has the entity been trading, is it the entity that actually produces, is there any recent change of ownership or registered address, and does the factory's scale make it plausible that it can absorb your programme? Discontinuity in the trading entity is a surprisingly common and easily missed signal, and it is one of the reasons to verify the invoicing entity against the production entity before placing any order.

Compliance and documentation depth (weight 10%). Can the supplier produce the certificates covering your specific products, and does it have the record systems to support them? Documentation depth is a reasonable proxy for operational maturity, and it is the dimension that determines whether a switch is administratively possible at speed.

Relationship depth and communication quality (weight 10%). How quickly does the supplier respond, does it give realistic rather than optimistic lead times, and does it raise problems proactively rather than at the point of failure? A supplier that tells you early that a colour will be four days late is materially more useful in a disruption than one that tells you on the due date.

Using the score. Score every supplier on the same scale and keep the results current, at minimum annually and after any significant event. The score is not an end in itself. Its value is that it makes the argument about backup capacity concrete. When a buyer says to a primary supplier that a second supplier is 40 percent of the volume and could step up within a defined window, the primary supplier has a clear incentive to protect the relationship, and the buyer has a genuinely tested option. Score the backup at least as rigorously as the primary. A backup supplier assessed to a lower standard is an assumption, not a plan.

Writing a contingency plan that can be executed in days

Most contingency plans are documents. Useful contingency plans are sequences. The difference is whether someone could pick up the document on the day the disruption happens and act on it without a meeting. A ribbon contingency plan should cover five things, each of which needs a name against it and a time attached.

The trigger. Define what starts the plan, and make it objective. A supplier missing a confirmed milestone date by more than three working days, a declared inability to meet a required in-stock date, or a material change in a construction or dye source all qualify. Vague triggers produce hesitation, and hesitation is the most expensive part of a supply disruption.

The escalation path. Who contacts whom, in what order, and by what channel? Include the commercial contact, the technical contact and the escalation contact, because a disruption frequently has a commercial dimension and a technical dimension that need to be resolved in parallel. Pre-agree a direct communication channel with your backup supplier, so that a transfer request does not begin with a cold introduction while the clock is running.

The transfer sequence. Write down, as a checklist, what has to happen for the backup to take over the programme: artwork and screen files transferred, colour standard sent and acknowledged, a strike-off produced and approved, material confirmed, production slot booked, inspection arranged, shipping booked. Every step has a realistic duration, and the sum of those durations is the real recovery time. Buyers who have never done this exercise usually discover a recovery time of three to five weeks, which is exactly the figure that determines how much buffer stock is needed.

The commercial switch. Set out how the backup supplier is engaged: which entity is instructed, how the deposit is handled, whether pricing is held or renegotiated, and how the timing of the balance changes. A defined price at a defined time is materially cheaper than a spot purchase in a crisis, and agreeing the number in advance is far easier than agreeing it at the moment of failure.

The customer-facing plan. If the programme is seasonal and the disruption cannot be resolved inside the window, the buyer needs a decision about the product itself: launch with a substitute trim, launch with a reduced colour range, hold the launch, or substitute a stock ribbon already in the supply chain. That decision has to be made at a level above the procurement desk, and it needs a threshold set in advance so it is not a debate conducted in the last week of the season. Having a stock ribbon in a compatible colour and width already approved and held is the cheapest insurance in this entire article, and it is the option that converts an existential problem into an inconvenience.

Rehearsing it. A plan that has never been executed is a document. Run at least one dry exercise a year, where you take a hypothetical disruption through the sequence on paper with the people who would have to do it. The exercise almost always reveals that a step has no owner or that two steps conflict, and it costs a couple of hours. This is the step that separates the buyers who have a plan from the buyers who have had a plan.

Protecting quality and colour consistency across two suppliers

Running the same specification in two places creates a variable that did not exist before, and it has to be managed rather than hoped about. The objective is not that both suppliers produce an identical roll, which is not physically achievable across dye lots. The objective is that the difference sits inside a tolerance that your own downstream process can absorb and that your customer will not notice.

Define the tolerance explicitly and numerically. Use a colour difference metric with a stated limit, such as Delta E with an agreed threshold, and specify it in the purchase order along with the method of measurement and the instrument used. "Colour to match" without a number is not a specification, and it is the clause that makes a dispute unresolvable because each party is measuring a different thing. The tolerance should also specify the conditions, because the same ribbon reads differently under different light sources, and a daylight booth with a defined illuminant removes an entire category of argument.

Hold a physical standard, not a photograph. A screen photograph of a colour is worthless as a standard because every display renders it differently. You need a sealed physical swatch from the approved bulk production, retained by the buyer and by each supplier, and every new dye lot is compared against it. Sealed, because ribbon fades and soil over time, and a standard that has degraded turns every future comparison into an argument about which version is correct.

Require a strike-off per dye lot. Each supplier submits a strike-off against the standard for each new dye lot before bulk production is authorised. This adds days to the schedule and it is the reason a switch takes longer than a phone call. Budget for it explicitly in the contingency timeline rather than discovering it during the disruption.

Set inspection equivalence across both lanes. The inspection standard, the sample sizes and the acceptance thresholds should be identical in both lanes, and the same inspection regime should apply whether the goods are in the primary's lane or the secondary's. A backup that is inspected to a lower standard because it is the backup is not a backup, it is a different product with a different risk profile.

Use the primary's production data to calibrate the secondary. Once the secondary starts running, compare their measured quality data: how many metres or units were rejected, what the recurring defects are, and how the rate changes over successive orders. Early in a relationship, the second supplier's rejection rate is typically higher, and the trend matters more than the absolute number. A steadily falling rejection rate means the construction is being learned properly. A flat rate suggests the capability is not there.

Decide how much variation your product can absorb. Some applications are brutally unforgiving, and a ribbon that forms the visible face of a gift box cannot vary. Others are forgiving, because the ribbon is one of several elements and the eye does not isolate it. Before splitting a SKU, establish how much colour and finish variation your product actually tolerates in market, because that number determines how many SKUs can be safely dual-sourced at all. It is also useful commercially: if a SKU is shade-critical, saying so explicitly to your supplier may change the quotation in a way that makes single-sourcing it honestly cheaper.

Questions worth asking any ribbon supplier about supply chain resilience

These are the questions that reveal whether a supplier has actually thought about the resilience of your programme, rather than simply yours.

  • Which raw materials do you buy from single-source suppliers, and what is the lead time on replenishment of each?
  • Which steps in our production route are subcontracted, and can we verify those suppliers ourselves?
  • What is your current utilisation on the lines that would run our construction, and what is the committed order book in our peak months?
  • If we asked you to absorb a 40 percent volume increase in four weeks, what would have to change?
  • What is the minimum order quantity for our construction at our width, and how flexible is it if we commit to a programme?
  • How do you ensure colour consistency between dye lots, and can we see your lab dip and strike-off approval process?
  • Do you hold any finished or WIP stock of our constructions, and for how long after an order is cancelled?
  • Which certifications cover our specific products and colourways, and can we verify them directly with the issuing body?
  • What is your policy on holding our artwork, screens and colour standards, and how are they transferred or destroyed?
  • How do you communicate when a milestone will slip, and how much notice will we get?
  • What would you need from us to run a small production trial against our full specification?
  • If we needed to move part of this programme elsewhere in future, what information would you be willing to release to make that possible?

FAQ — Custom ribbon supply chain resilience and multi-sourcing

Why should I dual source custom ribbon when a single supplier is cheaper?

A single source is cheaper only in the narrow sense of setup and administration cost, and that saving is measured against a risk whose real cost is not captured in any of your normal accounts. If a supplier fails during a seasonal window, the loss is the entire retail period for that product, not the ribbon's share of the bill of materials. A second source does not have to be used at equal volume; a qualified backup with an approved sample and a tested production trial covers most of the exposure at a fraction of the cost of splitting the programme evenly. The defensible position is to qualify a second supplier, run a production trial against it, and decide the split volume based on what the disruption would actually cost you.

How do I get a second ribbon supplier to accept a lower minimum order quantity?

Trade commitment rather than price. Secondary suppliers are generally far more willing to reduce a minimum than to reduce a unit price, because a lower minimum changes their order economics while a discount simply reduces their margin. What you can offer in exchange is a longer programme, a forecast you commit to honour, a smaller first trial in exchange for a reduced standing minimum, or consolidated shipping that lowers their handling cost per order. The supplier will also be more receptive once it has seen your production trial succeed, because at that point it has evidence about its own capability and the risk of your programme becomes much easier for it to price. Set the minimum discussion at the point of the trial order rather than at the point of the first commercial negotiation.

How long does it take to transfer a custom ribbon programme to a second supplier?

Realistically three to five weeks from the decision to a shipment, and the components that determine it are artwork and tooling transfer, a strike-off against the colour standard, approval of that strike-off, sourcing the yarn or film stock in the correct colour, booking the production slot and then the shipping slot. The two longest and most commonly underestimated steps are the strike-off approval, because it requires a dye lot and a physical sample, and the material sourcing, because a new colourway may need a dye batch that runs on its own cycle. This is why buffer inventory matters: the recovery time is the reason a four to eight week buffer on the critical colourway is the minimum sensible protection for a seasonal launch, and why the exercise of writing down each step with its duration should be done before you need it rather than during a disruption.

Will a second supplier be able to match the colour of my current ribbon?

Not to the same roll, and a good buyer plans for that rather than being disappointed by it. Two dye houses producing the same Pantone reference from the same fibre will land within a defined commercial tolerance but not identically, because dye lots, water chemistry, finishing tension and finishing route all vary. The practical answer is to define the tolerance numerically using a metric such as Delta E with a stated threshold, to hold a sealed physical swatch from the approved bulk production as the standard, to require a strike-off against that standard for every new dye lot, and to agree the measurement conditions and instrument in the purchase order. If your product is shade-critical across a wide range, test the secondary on the most exposed colourway first, because the tolerance that matters commercially is the one set by your weakest product, not by your average product.

What is the difference between a qualified backup supplier and a true dual-source arrangement?

A qualified backup has been evaluated, sampled, approved and documented, but has not yet produced commercially, so it is untested at your volume and its recovery time is longer and less certain. A true dual-source arrangement has both suppliers running live production from the same specification, so both are continuously rehearsing the programme and either can step up quickly. The backup is cheaper to establish and prevents a total stoppage; the dual-source arrangement preserves the schedule and protects negotiating position. For most buyers the sensible sequence is to establish a qualified backup first, since it addresses the concentration risk immediately at low cost, and then move the most critical SKUs to a live split as the volume and the relationship justify it.

How do I assess the supply chain risk of a ribbon manufacturer I have not audited in person?

Start with the input chain rather than the factory. Ask which raw materials are single-sourced, whether the yarn or film mill relationship is exclusive, what the replenishment lead time is on each critical input, and which steps of the production route are subcontracted rather than in-house. Then examine capacity headroom, because a supplier running at full book in your peak months cannot absorb a transferred volume, which is the entire purpose of a backup. Check the production entity against the invoicing entity, and confirm that the certificates covering your product and colourway are valid with the issuing body. Where a visit is possible, prioritise the dye house and the subcontracted printing step over the weaving floor.

What buffer stock should I hold for a seasonal ribbon programme?

Enough to cover the period between discovering a disruption and getting a replacement order into production, which is generally four to eight weeks of demand for the most critical construction. Buffer stock is also the only approach that removes the supplier's lead time from the critical path entirely, since a re-order still requires a strike-off approval, a dye lot and a production slot. It is worth noting that the buffer does not need to cover the whole programme. Concentrating it on the top two or three critical colourways or constructions, rather than spreading it thinly across a wide range, buys more protection for less money. For a programme where the ribbon is the reason the product exists, a modest buffer on the critical items is the cheapest insurance available and it is the option that turns a supply failure into a stock adjustment rather than a cancelled launch.

How should I segment my ribbon programme when deciding what to dual source?

Rank the programme by criticality rather than by volume, and split only the top tier. Criticality is best measured as the revenue at risk if that specific SKU stops, which is usually a small number of colourways or constructions carrying most of the programme's exposure. Applying a blanket split across every SKU in a range is the most common mistake, because it multiplies the fixed setup cost of a second source, including separate printing setup and separate colour standards, without adding meaningful protection. The right structure is a live split on the two or three critical items, a qualified backup across the remainder of the range, and a buffer stock concentrated on the single most exposed construction. This structure also gives you a clear internal story when you present the resilience budget to a finance stakeholder, because each layer has a measurable purpose.

What happens to my artwork and screens if I move production to a second supplier?

This has to be arranged in advance, because the transfer is blocked without it and discovering the problem during a disruption is the worst possible timing. The buyer should own the artwork files, the print screens or plates and the physical colour standard, and the agreement should require them to be returned or destroyed on written request rather than retained indefinitely. Most practical problems with transferring to a second supplier are not about the supplier being unwilling but about nobody having established who physically holds the tooling, in what condition, and how it gets shipped. Keep a registered copy of every print file in your own system, have the supplier mark every screen and strike-off with your reference, and ask for a photographic record of the tooling at the start of each production run so that the condition is documented before it matters. Suppliers who object to this arrangement are telling you something important about how they will treat your intellectual property in a dispute.
